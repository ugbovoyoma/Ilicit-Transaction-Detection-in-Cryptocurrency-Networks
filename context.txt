INTRODUCTION
Background of the Study
Over the past two decades, a significant shift to digital services has greatly changed the global system of finance. Major among these transformations are cryptocurrency networks and blockchains, which promote the principles of decentralisation, openness, and efficiency in making financial transactions (Nakamoto, 2008). Cryptocurrency networks consist of distributed nodes (computers), blockchain protocols, and cryptographic consensus mechanism which operate to maintain a common, unalterable ledger of transactions (Yaga et al., 2019). Cryptocurrency networks are distinct from cryptocurrencies themselves, which represent the actual digital assets (e.g., Bitcoin, Ethereum) traded across such networks (Khan and Hakami, 2022). 
The term cryptocurrency is a combination of the words ‘cryptography’ and ‘currency’. Cryptography originated from an ancient Greek terminology and was used to refer to secret or cryptic writing systems. This name demonstrates the main idea behind these digital assets; the use of high-level encryption processes to ensure that the financial transactions are safe without the involvement of a third party. (Bongini et al, 2025). Bitcoin, the very first cryptocurrency, helped launch the field of decentralised finance (DeFi), alternative cryptocurrencies (altcoins), and NFT markets because it created a peer-to-peer electronic cash system that did not depend on financial intermediaries (Bongini et al, 2025). Since transactions are recorded on a blockchain, a decentralised technology, they become unchangeable and verifiable, promoting trust in a system where no one trusts you by default (Fandl, 2019). Due to this architectural decentralisation, there are lower transaction costs, quicker international payment processing, and better financial opportunities for people who live in remote areas (Chen and Bellavitis, 2019). The value of cryptocurrencies in the global market has now exploded, and thousands of active users presently participate in them. Along with big investments from institutions, digital currencies are becoming a common feature in many international markets (Stevanović et al, 2023; Philip and Pandey, 2024).
However, the same characteristics that provide cryptocurrency networks their disruptive power such as anonymity or pseudonymity for users (where individuals are represented and identified using cryptography addresses instead of their verified personal details), their global scope, the high speed of transactions, and the absence of main governing bodies, at the same time create tough challenges (Silva and Ferreira, 2020). Even though these attributes are praised for promoting privacy and operational efficiency, they are sometimes exploited by malicious entities, including fraudsters, ransomware attackers, terrorist financers, and sanction evaders due to the unregulated, decentralised nature of the system (De Filippi, 2016). In this context, the problem of identifying illicit activities in cryptocurrency networks is becoming urgent (Turner et al., 2020). 
The term illicit transactions refer to blockchain-related transfers linked to unlawful activity, or violation of regulations. These examples of illegal activities consist of, and go beyond, money laundering; paying for terrorism; several types of fraud, such as Ponzi schemes and phishing; paying ransoms to hackers; and evading sanctions (Financial Action Task Force, 2019; Gandal et al., 2018). Because of how large and complex these networks are, and because transactions happen across multiple borders, traditional financial crime detection methods are not very effective and respond only to problems after they arise (Moreno et al, 2021). While banks must comply with strict laws and controls and keep clear records of their transactions, e.g., Know Your Customer (KYC) and Anti-Money Laundering (AML) protocols, cryptocurrency transactions can quickly happen between people of many nations, often making them extremely difficult to connect to specific identities (Dyson et al, 2019). Because of the low transparency of these systems, law enforcement and regulators find it very challenging to watch and track illicit financial activities. The quick development of methods of concealing transaction origins such as mixers, tumblers, and privacy-enhancing cryptocurrencies further complicates this challenge (Dyson et al, 2019). 
The increasing risk of illegal financial activity on cryptocurrency networks is a significant concern for the world, which has led international organisations to demand prompt action (Financial Action Task Force, 2021). The Financial Action Task Force (FATF), among other organisations, has provided detailed guidelines outlining how critical it is to manage AML and Counter-Terrorist Financing (CFT) measures within the crypto asset sector (Financial Action Task Force, 2021). The cryptocurrency industry must demonstrate that financial crime does not compromise its growth and respectability so as to enable long term integrity, credibility and widespread adoption (Teichmann and Falker, 2021). When illegal transactions are not effectively noticed and controlled, such behaviour decreases public trust, stops mainstream institutions from using the system, and introduces large-scale dangers to a nation’s safety and the stability of finance around the globe (Walton and Dhillon, 2017; Boyko et al., 2022). The high-profile and well-publicised instances of massive hacks, ingenious scams such as QuadrigaCX and OneCoin, and the use of digital assets for illicit purposes as witnessed in WannaCry and Colonial Pipeline testify to the fact that cyber-related financial risks are becoming more advanced and serious (International Monetary Fund, 2024; Haentjens, 2020; Zhang, 2019). With the surge of heavy, rapid, and varied cryptocurrency transactions, distinguishing legitimacy from illicit activities has become very complex, which calls for the use of advanced tools (Zhao et al, 2023). 
Historically, detecting unlawful use of money in traditional banks mainly involved using set rules and basic statistical analysis (Bolton and Hand, 2002). Since rule-based systems follow strict guidelines like flagging transactions that exceeds a certain monetary value or unusual transfer frequencies, these techniques display a certain level of performance in stable, well-regulated and well-known financial environments, they are fundamentally flawed and soon obsolete in face of the rapidly evolving, decentralised and dynamic environments of cryptocurrency networks making it easily bypassed by tearaway criminals who can easily adapt their methods to known detection parameters (Kim et al., 2021). Transactions on blockchains happen so fast and in large numbers, it is not practical to check them manually, not even when the rules determine when they need to be reviewed (Dyson et al, 2019). Mainly, classical models struggle to account for the graph-like structure present in transaction data where the important details are found in the links between several parties rather than in separate or isolated transactions. Typically, these models process data only at a fixed point and cannot detect vital temporal patterns; how suspicious trends evolve, gather, and spread through a set time, or how several separate transactions turn into something more suspicious over the course of several days or weeks (Li et al. 2022). To overcome these limitations, there must be a significant change toward using flexible, intelligent, and advanced analytical frameworks. With the faster growth and wider use of Machine Learning (ML) an area of artificial intelligence that allows systems to use given data to spot patterns that it then uses to make a decision or prediction, without it being explicitly programmed and Deep Learning (DL) an expert branch of ML, which partially applies multi-layered neural networks to automatically discover hierarchical representation of complex data, it is now possible to handle these challenges effectively. ML and DL models are now able to understand and extract value from data with many dimensions and structures without assuming anything about how the data is distributed, its linearity, or relationships (LeCun et al., 2015; Goodfellow et al., 2016).
The structure of cryptocurrency transactions is naturally based on graphs, where individual addresses are represented by nodes, and the value transfer (by crypto) is represented by edges going from one node to another (Wu et al., 2020; Maesa et al., 2019). In many cases, illicit activities within the network manifest as specific groupings that are difficult to identify from individual transactions alone (Akcora et al., 2018), such as large concentrations of laundering services, complex laundering networks, or tightly connected clusters of high-risk entities. The traditional method of displaying data in a flat table fails to adequately represent these essential relational aspects and context of the broader network. Furthermore, the sequential recording of blockchain transactions with timestamps underscores the importance of detecting timing trends. Rapid changes in wallets, unusual timings for transactions, and similar movements of funds between several wallets are some of the many features of illicit crypto activities found by researchers (Li et al., 2022; Elmougy.,2023; Tharani et al., 2021). In most cases, existing literature studies these crucial topics (transaction graph representations and temporal dynamics) alone or combines them in basic ways, overlooking the strong results possible when they are considered as a set (Hiraoka et al, 2020). There is a strong and pressing need for innovative ways to integrate two fundamental dimensions: the evolution of transactions over time “temporal patterns” and the complex web of node-level relationships between participants “graph-based analysis” (Li et al., 2018).
To handle these issues, it is necessary to develop techniques that can identify entire criminal networks, predict their growth, characterise their operational patterns, and monitor suspicious activities (Turner et al., 2020). Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are a new and quickly growing field of deep learning that is specifically made to work with graph-structured data (Hamilton et al., 2017). GNNs are exceptionally adept at learning powerful node and edge representations by recursively aggregating and transforming information from their local neighbourhoods within the graph (Kipf and Welling, 2016). In the same way, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), along with advanced ones like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, are especially useful for finding sequential and time-based patterns in streaming data such as cryptocurrency transaction flows (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; Goodfellow et al., 2016). Combining all these capabilities (how they are structured and how they change over time) enables us to build robust models that adapt to the intricate structure of criminal networks and their dynamic activities. Although, despite some successful tries to identify fraud in cryptocurrencies by existing literature utilising isolated structural or temporary models, this proposed study considers an integrated model, where the topology of the graph and sequential behavioural patterns are learnt simultaneously using both Graph Neural Network and Recurrent Neural Network. Such an approach of two-modelling presents a more holistic way of tracking illegal activity in decentralised networks.
This study therefore seeks to make use of the most advanced techniques in ML and DL, with specific attention on GNNs and RNNs/LSTMs, to greatly improve the accuracy, protection, and speed when spotting illicit transactions in cryptocurrency networks. A critical resource for this challenging endeavour is the publicly available Elliptic Dataset (Weber et al., 2019). The dataset offers an extensive summary of Bitcoin transactions in the form of directed lines where each node connects two transactions and represents how cryptocurrency moves between them. Importantly, it contains real labels for both legal and illegal deals and over 166 features about each deal or transaction based on different time steps (Weber et al., 2019). Because this dataset presents researchers insights into temporal activity, connected nodes, and labels, it offers a solid framework for advancing and thoroughly testing models that join graph and temporal concepts in their analysis.
A range of approaches have been investigated in recent work using the Elliptic dataset, including temporal feature modelling, anomaly detection, and supervised graph learning using Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) and Graph Attention Networks (GATs). Nevertheless, there is one major challenge that is impeding this effective and efficient combination of relational structure, which is the relationships amongst the data points, the temporal patterns, and the variation of the data over time, which is an outstanding subject (Weber et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2025). This research therefore seeks to go beyond the classical statistical and machine learning methods and contribute to the enhancement of the credibility and security of the modern digital financial systems across the world, especially given that many challenges remain in efficiently identifying concealed and dynamic criminal activity within rapidly evolving cryptocurrency networks; which is the primary focus of this study. 
Statement of the Problem
The extreme popularity and rapid adoption of cryptocurrency networks and their subsequent integration into the global financial system have both created new opportunities to innovate and presented threats to financial security (Kurshan et al., 2020). The increasing size and complexity of these illegal activities pose an extreme and rising risk to the integrity and stability of not only the emerging digital asset market but also the global financial system as a whole (Foley et al., 2019). Existing detection mechanisms mostly borrowed from the traditional finance sector, have been ineffective for cryptocurrency networks. Adaptive actors can easily get around rule-based systems which commonly have high false positives and negatives (Acevedo-Viloria et al., 2021; Pan, 2024; Kadam, 2024). Despite the flexibility provided by machine learning, the majority are implemented on flat-table data (Borisov et al., 2022) and are, thus, incapable of making use of temporal dynamics and graph-based relationships, which are two key factors in illicit activity in blockchain environments (Li et al., 2018; Kurshan and Shen, 2020). Transactions in cryptocurrency take place over time and along intricate relational structures, constituting dynamic networks and webs of interrelated agents or parties (Akcora et al., 2018). However, these dimensions, time and structure, are highly interdependent in most models, missing the critical interrelatedness between these dimensions. An example is that they may flag a single suspicious transaction but fail to detect a temporally coordinated laundering cascade or structurally anomalous fraud ring (Li et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020). The large size and speed of transactions also make manual checkups and basic analytics impossible (Sanka and Cheung, 2021). Despite the potential of combining sequential models (e.g., LSTMs) plus graph-based representations (e.g., GNNs), such hybrid architecture remains underexplored, mostly due to model complexity, computational expenses, and a scarcity of benchmark datasets that contain temporal and structural annotations (Wu et al., 2020). Another factor is that a great number of models are black boxes, with low interpretability, which is an unaccepted limitation in regulated areas, such as anti-money laundering (AML), where legal defensibility and auditability are of paramount importance. Moreover, the class imbalance issue, i.e., transactions of illicit acts are a minority, produces biased models that discriminate against the legitimate class and degrade the detection performance (Weber et al., 2019). Lastly, the great majority of academic models are designed and tested under siloed conditions and are poor at scaling to high-utilisation, low-latency blockchain networks such as Bitcoin. They can easily produce bad performance in production. As it is argued by Paleyes et al. (2020), the domain is in dire need of transparent, adaptive, and operationally realistic detection frameworks relying on real-world complexity. 
This study aims to fill these essential gap areas by developing hybrid machine and deep learning models that integrate temporal sequences with graph-based learning (e.g., LSTMs with GNNs). Evaluated on the Elliptic dataset for accuracy, precision, explainability, and real-world practicability in detecting illicit payments (Kurshan and Shen, 2020; Li et al., 2018). This study presents a unified framework to capture the interdependence of temporal or structural signals as compared to the previous methods that present a single approach in isolation. It is one of the very first to apply such hybrid models to public blockchain data with emphasis on scalability, interpretability and reproducibility (Wu et al., 2020; Weber et al., 2019). Importantly, this research addresses a serious gap within the region. There has been a lot of research conducted in the Global North about cryptocurrency abuse; however, in sub-Saharan Africa, it has been given minimal attention, especially since its adoption is increasing and regulatory policies are weak (Boyode, 2024). If overlooked, this poses a global risk: the continued inability of enforcement agencies and blockchain analytics firms to detect illicit flows using interpretable, real-time systems enabling bad actors to operate with impunity (Dyson et al., 2019; Paleyes et al., 2020).
Objectives of the Study
The primary objective of this study is to develop and evaluate machine and deep learning models with strong robustness in detecting illicit transactions in cryptocurrency networks by synergistically exploiting both temporal and graph-based features.
The specific objectives are:
To analyse and detect illicit activities within the structural (graph-based) and temporal characteristics of the Elliptic cryptocurrency transaction dataset by uncovering patterns that reflect suspicious behaviours.
To develop high-resolution and informative features that could effectively capture subtle temporary patterns (such as transaction sequences and frequency over time) and evolving topological features (such as propagation pathways, community structures and centrality) in the cryptocurrency transaction graph.
To train and compare the performance of different traditional machine learning algorithms (including logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting) on identifying illegal transactions and set them as good baseline models.
To develop and extensively evaluate state-of-the-art deep learning models, namely Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) or Long Short-Term Memory (LSTMs) to model temporal dependencies, and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to utilise structural information in the context of detecting illicit transactions.
To develop and apply new hybrid machine and deep learning architectures that combine effectively both temporal and graph-based features to improve illicit transaction detection, aiming to capitalise on the complementary strengths of these data dimensions.
To compare the developed models in an extensive manner according to the existing performance measures (e.g., precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, average precision), putting a special focus on the measures that are applicable to highly imbalanced datasets.
1.4 Research Questions
The following research questions are presented to guide the investigation in line with the background, problem statement, and objectives outlined in this research. These questions are designed to investigate the existing knowledge and methodological gaps of illicit transaction detection in cryptocurrency networks with a special emphasis on the use of temporal patterns and graph-based structures with the help of sophisticated machine and deep learning techniques.
In what ways do illegal practices occur within the temporal and graph-based dimensions of cryptocurrency transactional networks? The question aims to ascertain the behavioural fingerprints and latent features of illicit financial flows, concentrating not only on sequential (time-based) but also relational (graph-structured) patterns of transactions. 
What are the shortcomings of current rule-based and machine learning models for identifying illicit transactions in pseudonymous, decentralised, and extensive throughput blockchain environments? The aim is to critically assess the drawbacks of traditional detection systems in identifying complex and dynamic illicit behaviours, particularly under conditions of class imbalance and structural complexity of data. 
What aspects are needed to design comprehensive and informative features that could ensure capturing both dynamic temporal patterns (e.g., transaction sequences and activity bursts) and topological graph properties (e.g., centrality and community structure) that can be found in blockchain transaction data? The question touches on the methodological issue of how to transform raw data on blockchains into meaningful inputs to learn interpretable models more accurately.
How far can traditional machine learning algorithms (including but not limited to logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting) be used as effective baselines for detecting illegal cryptocurrency transactions? This approach includes benchmarking conventional models to get the basic comparison on performance before one uses the more advanced deep learning techniques.
What is the best practice for using deep learning models like recurrent neural networks (RNNs), long-short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and graph neural networks (GNNs) to capture both the time and structural complexity of illicit transaction behaviour? In this question, the potential of time-series and graph-based architectures for learning meaningful representations of blockchain data is explored.
How do hybrid deep learning architectures that combine temporal sequence learning and graph-based relational learning influence the accuracy, recall, and robustness of models to detect illicit transactions? This central question checks whether the combined use of GNNs and RNNs or LSTMs has a better performance due to the ability to identify synergistic patterns in data.
What performance metrics (precision, recall, F1-score, AUC-ROC, average precision) are the most appropriate in evaluating illicit transaction detection in highly imbalanced cryptocurrency data, and how do the suggested models perform on them? This discussion facilitates intensive model calibration and assists in defining which metrics are most revealing in situations where compliance is a sensitive issue. 
What interpretability techniques, such as SHAP and LIME, can provide actionable explanations for the best-performing models, and how might they be useful in conducting forensic and compliance investigations? This question serves as a bridge between the model's effectiveness and its applicability in the real world, ensuring that the outputs are clear and understandable to professionals in the field.
What is the scalability, flexibility, and efficacy of the prescribed hybrid detection model when implemented in real-life financial compliance, forensic analysis, or regulatory technology settings? The last question focuses on the operational viability of the research and its generalisability in areas outside the academic context.
Overall, these questions are the basis of the study. They fill in important knowledge gaps and also fit directly to the specific objectives set in Section 1.3. In combination, they provide coherent direction in designing, developing, evaluating, and interpreting models all through the research process.

1.6 Scope of the Study
This study revolves around modelling machine learning and deep learning applications to detect illegal transactions in cryptocurrency systems. The study falls into the interdisciplinary field of data science, cybersecurity, artificial intelligence, blockchain analytics, and financial crime detection. This study only focuses on detecting illegal financial dealings or activities, like money laundering, scams, and fraud, as evident in Bitcoin transactions. It does not cover other types of cybercrime, such as ransomware, identity theft, or phishing, unless it is presented as an unusual transaction pattern in the dataset. The research follows a quantitative approach and uses supervised learning methods. It uses various models as baselines, including logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosting. The study also incorporates more sophisticated models that account for temporal behaviours like recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks. Additionally, graph neural networks (GNNs) are used to analyse structural dependencies or relationships among transactions. The study also explores mixed models that combine temporal and structural learning so as to improve the accuracy of predictions when classifying transactions as either licit or illicit.
The study is based only on the publicly available Elliptic Dataset, which contains more than 200,000 transactions of Bitcoin. The transactions themselves are treated as nodes in a directed graph, and edges represent the flow of money. In every transaction, there are 165 anonymous features, and there are three possible labels attached to them: "licit", "illicit", and "unknown". Also offered is temporal information for 49 time steps, which makes it possible to model sequential transaction behaviour. No external or proprietary data from exchanges, wallets, or financial institutions is used in this research. The technical aspect of this work is implemented using open-source technologies. Data processing is done with Python, classical machine learning is done with Scikit-learn, and deep learning is done with PyTorch or TensorFlow. PyTorch Geometric or Deep Graph Library (DGL) is employed in graph-based learning, while SHAP or LIME helps to support model interpretability and explainability. All model training and evaluation activities are carried out or conducted offline, and the study does not involve deployment of real-time or production-ready detection systems.
Since the dataset does not relate to a particular country or region, the results are aimed to be universal. The study is also consistent with international policies for enhancing anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorism financing (CFT) efforts in accordance with the advocacy of international organisations like the Financial Action Task Force (FATF). However, the research does not explore country-based legal or regulatory frameworks. The study itself is also restricted to Bitcoin-based transactional data and does not extend to other cryptocurrencies like Ethereum, Monero, or Zcash. Unsupervised learning, anomaly detection, and visualisation of networks are not applied or used. Also, the study does not include qualitative data from interviews or expert consultation. Nevertheless, the study is expected to deliver findings applicable and beneficial to regulators, investigators, and practitioners in digital financial security.
1.7 Operational Definition of Key Terms
The following definitions are accorded to these terms so as to offer uniformity and understanding throughout the study. 
Black-box Model: A machine learning model whose internal framework is opaque and not interpretable by a human, usually due to its complex form (e.g., deep neural networks), which makes its predictions very difficult to interpret directly.
Blockchain: An immutable, decentralised, distributed and digital ledger system that allows transactions to be recorded safely across a network of computers. 
Class Imbalance: A characteristic of a classification dataset in which the number of observations in a single category (the majority class) is much greater than the number of observations in another category (the minority class), which makes training the model difficult.
Cryptocurrency: Cryptocurrency is a type of decentralised digital currency guaranteed by cryptography allowing peer-to-peer transaction with no intermediaries and central authorities required.
Cryptocurrency Networks: Cryptocurrency systems that use cryptography to secure digital or virtual currencies to enable peer-to-peer payments and typically operate as decentralised systems. It operates without the control of a central bank or any government agency.
Deep Learning (DL): A branch of machine learning based on artificial neural networks with multiple layers (deep neural networks) to learn high-dimensional patterns and multi-layered hierarchical representations directly in the raw data. 
Edge (in graph context): A connection or a path between two nodes of a graph, e.g., a flow of cryptocurrency between transactions or addresses within a network.
Graph-Based Analysis: Use of graph theory to model and analyse relationships and structure in a data set, particularly successful in interpreting the interrelationships among the objects in networks such as the graph of transactions.
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): An architecture of deep learning, particularly developed to operate over graph-structured data, able to learn strong node and edge representations through aggregation and transformation of the local information in the graph. 
Hyperparameters: Parameters that are configured prior to the learning process and that define the general form and conduct of the learning algorithm, instead of being inferred from the data itself (e.g., learning rate, the number of hidden layers, the number of trees of a random forest).
Illicit Transactions: The equivalent of financial crimes in cryptocurrency networks. They are unlawful, deceptive, or malevolent, including criminal undertakings like money laundering, financing terrorism, or fraud. 
Latent Features: Non-obvious, unobservable properties or aspects of data which unsupervised or semi-supervised models (such as NMF or SVD) learn to represent the underlying structure of data in a lower-dimensional space.
Machine Learning (ML): A sub-field of artificial intelligence that enables computer systems to learn, automatically improve performance and make decisions based on data. It finds patterns and uncovers facts without being explicitly programmed.
Node (in graph context): A single entity or point on a graph, which might be, e.g., a constant transaction or a cryptocurrency account on a network.
Optimisation Algorithm: A process of computation commonly applied in machine learning to optimise the set of parameters of a model, done by successively minimising some specific loss or cost function.
Pseudonymity: The feature of crypto transactions that the identity of the participants in a transaction is obfuscated with a cryptographic address, publicly visible but not directly identifying real-world personal data, providing some anonymity to each transaction.
Temporal Patterns: Patterns, trends, or other behaviours of data that develop over time and show the dynamics of phenomena occurring (e.g., abnormal timing, frequency, or coordinated movements of cryptocurrency transactions).

LITERATURE REVIEW AND THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
2.1 Introduction
The introduction of digital currencies has revolutionised the financial market with the advantage of decentralised, open ledger transactions. Even though blockchain has a public record, cryptographic addresses are anonymous, and it is difficult to determine the parties responsible for dubious actions. This chapter is an overview of methods used in identifying illegal financial activities in cryptocurrency networks, particularly Bitcoin. It reviews data modelling and empirical machine and deep learning studies especially on graph-based and time series models (GNNs and LSTMs). It highlights bottlenecks in scaling temporal and topological features and, therefore, urges more flexible, interpretable, and adaptable models. The goal is to support hybrid deep learning frameworks integrating graph-based sequential data to provide better detection and interpretation of illegal transactions in financial data.
2.2 Review of Concepts
This section presents the definitions of concepts and terms that form the basis of understanding the context and extent of detecting illicit transactions in cryptocurrency networks. The concepts below are the theoretical and empirical backbone of this research.
2.2.1 Blockchain and Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT)
Blockchain is a form of distributed ledger technology that records blocks of data that are permanently linked and secured through cryptographic methods or techniques (Nakamoto, 2008). The blocks hold a timestamp, a cryptographic hash of the preceding block, as well as transaction data. It has a decentralised structure that avoids central authorities and thus improves transparency, security and fault tolerance. (Silva and Ferreira, 2020; Fandl, 2019).
More generally, distributed ledger technology (DLT) is defined as a database (see figure 2.1) that exists over numerous places and actors, where each party has an identical copy, and the replicas are updated and synchronised using a consensus mechanism (Romero, 2018). We can also say that distributed ledgers are generally implemented via a blockchain, a particular type of database in which individual transactions are processed and stored in interconnected groups or blocks organised in chronological order to create a chain (Romero, 2018). The integrity and security of the data stored in the chain are guaranteed using cryptography. Although blockchain is the best-known use of DLT, especially due to its use case of crypto-assets like bitcoin, other financial applications have also been developed in recent years (Romero, 2018). Nevertheless, the scalability of blockchain and transaction throughput is often deemed the most significant technical bottleneck, particularly when it comes to large-capacity financial systems. There are proposed solutions like sharding, the use of off-chain protocols (Lightning Network, etc.), and layer-2 solutions; they bring trade-offs between the degree of decentralisation, speed, and security (Zheng et al., 2018).
The benefits of DLT include a decrease in the costs of operations, improved traceability, and higher efficiency in the field of financial services. Blockchain is a particular application of DLT, which compiles data into cryptographically-linked blocks confirmed by consensus and provides immutability and audibility. Blockchains can be classified as either public (permissionless) blockchains or private (permissioned) blockchains depending on their access and control capabilities, where they present different transparency and control ratios (Romero, 2018).

2.2.2 Cryptocurrencies
Cryptocurrencies are digital or virtual currencies whose security and enactment are through cryptography and usually through blockchain technology (Nakamoto, 2008). Being the building blocks of the larger cryptocurrency ecosystem (see figure 2.2), these digital currencies enable peer-to-peer transactions without the use of more centralised third parties like bank institutions or payment processors (Sabry et al., 2020). Since its introduction in 2009, Bitcoin has been the best-known and most popular currency to use as an asset and a store of value (Gandal, 2018). The other most popular cryptocurrencies are Ethereum and Litecoin, as well as Monero (Sabry et al., 2020). These types of digital assets possess features such as privacy and decentralisation, but they also have security risks of misuse in illicit financial activities. Privacy-orientated cryptocurrencies like Monero and Zcash employ zero-knowledge proofs and ring signatures, which hide the identifications of a sender, a recipient, and a value transferred, making forensic analysis fairly challenging (Bohme et al., 2015). This issue has made regulators worry that they may be used in money laundering and financing terrorism.

2.2.3 Pseudonymity in Blockchain:
A significant characteristic of blockchain is that the users do not transact using their identifiable information but their cryptographic addresses. Such pseudonymity implies that the transaction details are publicly known, but the identity of the person or party to a transaction is usually unknown. This has generated space for privacy and even created difficulties for enforcement agencies that want to track illicit activity (Weber et al., 2019). Attackers can take advantage of this pseudonymity by using mixers, peel chains, and chain-hopping attacks that mix or obfuscate fund flows over many blockchains. These activities undermine the process of using heuristic clustering practices, making it very difficult to determine the ownership of addresses or the intent behind transactions (Feng et al., 2019).
 
2.2.4 Transaction Graphs:
A transaction graph is a mathematical description of cryptocurrency transactions, in which nodes are addresses, and edges describe a transfer of value between nodes (see figure 3). These graphs are directed and usually have complicated topologies that may be studied to identify dubious patterns (Akcora et al., 2018). However, transaction graphs may be very sparse or noisy, and illegal activity may be hidden in heavily connected cliques that fail to pass the conventional detection thresholds. Graph anomaly detection is still an open problem, especially in those cases where illicit patterns are temporally disjointed or adversarially hidden (Chen et al., 2025). Customary methods of exploring the structure of these networks include centrality measures, community detection and subgraph analysis (Tao et al., 2021).

2.2.5 Temporal Patterns in Transactions:
Temporal analysis is the inspection of the sequence and timing of transactions to determine the abnormal behaviours. Unlawful money flows commonly manifest distinct time features, including buying sprees, abnormal transaction times, or coordinated air transfer of finances to and fro addresses (Xu and Chen, 2020). Time-dependent features have the potential of displaying latent patterns that would not be detected using static analysis (Hiraoka et al., 2020). 
2.2.6 Machine Learning (ML):
ML is a term that describes a group of algorithms that allow systems to learn information presented in data and predict without the need for hard-coded programming. When it comes to the detection of illicit transactions, ML techniques like decision trees, support vector machines, and ensemble methods are used to detect patterns denoting fraudulent behaviours (Bolton and Hand, 2002). Nevertheless, traditional ML models tend to have the assumption of independent and identically distributed data stores, and this would reduce their ability to learn relational or sequential patterns in blockchain transactions. Also, these models could easily become overly fitted to data characterised by imbalanced class distributions that are typically found in datasets relating to illicit activities (Li et al., 2022).
2.2.7 Deep Learning (DL):
DL is an advanced branch of ML, which exploits multi-layered neural networks and learns high-level representations of large datasets. DL approaches are very useful in processing high-dimensional, sequential and unstructured data. Practical examples in this area are the application of deep neural networks in feature extraction and classification tasks (Goodfellow et al., 2016).
2.2.8 Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are an extremely well-performing family of deep learning-based architectures whose purpose is to perform inference on data objects that have natural representations as graphs. GNNs are able to process non-Euclidean structures – e.g., social networks, citation graphs, transportation systems, and cryptocurrency transaction networks – directly by exploiting their relational structure, unlike neural networks that require Euclidean data (e.g., images, text, time series) to process (Wu et al., 2020).
The fundamental concept of GNNs is message passing: the embedding (representation) of a graph node (e.g., a vertex or object) is determined by aggregating messages of the neighbours. Such is repeated on several layers, allowing each node to sequentially capture more global structural and contextual knowledge (Kipf and Welling, 2016; Hamilton et al., 2017). This process is shown in figure 2.4. The representations of nodes at one layer are combined with the features of the neighbouring nodes and sometimes with a non-linear activation function (e.g., ReLU), effectively changing them at subsequent layers. Embedding of each node incorporates information about a broader neighbourhood after numerous propagation steps. Although GNNs are powerful in representation, scaling up to dynamic graphs such as the ones used in real-time blockchain rings becomes a problem. Moreover, financial compliance applications often view them as black boxes due to their uninterpretability (Ying et al., 2019).

2.2.9 Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and LSTM Models: 
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are a type of artificial neural network focused on processing sequential and time-series data. Unlike conventional feedforward networks, RNNs possess cyclic connections able to possess a kind of memory. This is possible because the hidden states of the network are transmitted through time steps. They are particularly effective in applications where the timing of inputs holds vital information e.g. natural language processing, speech recognition, and, more importantly, the sequence of financial transactions (Goodfellow et al., 2016; Bengio et al., 1994).
In the case of cryptocurrency transactions, chains of payments containing identical addresses (or between interconnected addresses) can repeatedly indicate time-related behavioural patterns that relate to benign or unlawful actions. For example, frequent transactions performed by an account in a short period of time, long downtimes followed by a surge of activities, or systematic series of transfers can all generate money laundering suspicion, phishing, or mixing services (Li et al., 2022; Phua et al., 2010). Learning these kinds of patterns requires models that can learn based on sequences of variable length and can also retain context over time.
However, standard RNNs have limitations in their ability to model long sequences as a result of the vanishing gradient issue, where gradients shrink during backpropagation through time, thereby rendering the network unable to remember relevant information over long periods of intervals (Bengio et al., 1994). This is particularly undesirable when it comes to finding financial crime patterns with long-term temporal patterns, e.g., temporally dispersed but also semantically related transactions, e.g., layering in money laundering (Wan and Li, 2024).
In order to handle these issues, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) proposed the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, a specialised type of RNN that uses a memory cell that has the ability to retain information for a long period. There are three gating mechanisms which control this memory cell:
Input gate decides the extent or amount to which new input should be stored in the cell.
Forget gate chooses what has to be forgotten.
The output gate decides what part of the memory is passed to the next layer/time step.
This gating structure helps alleviate the problem of vanishing gradient since it allows the flow of information in a controlled manner, thereby avoiding the loss of important knowledge in longer sequences (Gers et al., 2000). Nevertheless, LSTMs can fail in cases of non-uniform event frequencies, as they often occur in transaction streams. To improve the encoding of asynchronous timing events, recent works suggest the use of time-aware LSTMs or attention-based temporal models (Nguyen et al., 2020).
LSTMs also possess two states: the hidden state (ℎ𝑡) and the cell state (𝑐𝑡), and this is how they are able to capture both the short-term and long-term contextual signals at the same time (see figure 2.5). This architecture has been particularly useful in the detection of transaction-based frauds where they require a model that would be able to recognise subtle changes in sequence behaviour over time (Phua et al., 2010).

2.2.10 Anomaly Detection: 
Anomaly detection is the process of detecting patterns in data that deviate significantly from the norm. It plays a central role in financial fraud detection whereby the aim is differentiating legitimate and suspicious transactions. Approaches include clustering, statistical models, autoencoders and one-class SVMs (Chandola et al., 2009).
2.2.11 Cryptocurrency vs. Conventional systems. 
Crypto, unlike normal banking, is global and has no borders. There is no central body or KYC (Know Your Customer) that is necessarily applied on-chain. Money is able to travel between jurisdictions at the speed of lightning (Dyson et al., 2019). These aspects prompted institutions, such as FATF, to apply the compliance of AML/CFT to virtual assets. Although the ratio of illegal volume is minimal, the volume requires new tools at the absolute scale (Financial Action Task Force, 2021). According to Pérez-Cano and Jurado, pseudonymity and the sophisticated nature of blockchain transactions have made traditional anomaly detection tools and methods largely ineffective, demanding a more advanced analysis (Pérez-Cano and Jurado, 2025). Moreover, the absence of central reporting, real-name identity protocols, or controlled gatekeepers makes it challenging to deploy old-school Know-Your-Customer (KYC) and AML systems (Dyson et al., 2019). Such discrepancy needs cross-discipline innovations, including the use of cryptography, graph analytics, and regulatory technology (regtech) innovations (De Filippi and Hassan, 2018).
Collectively, these notions furnish the background, both theoretical and technical, sufficient to address the intricacies surrounding the problem of identifying illegal transactions within the cryptocurrency networks. The next section expounds on the notions and investigates the types of data modelling techniques and tools that have been employed in the area.
2.3 Review of Data Modelling (DM) Techniques and Tools
Identifying illicit transactions in cryptocurrency systems requires a powerful and flexible data modelling method because blockchain environments are dynamic, pseudonymous and decentralised. Historically, blockchain modelling solutions have evolved from rule-based heuristics and statistical anomaly detection all the way to advanced machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) graph-based models capable of exploiting both temporal (time-based) and graph (relational-based) information in blockchain data. In this section, the review of these techniques is given in detail with highlights of their strengths, limitations, and applicability in decentralised systems in financial crime detection.

2.3.1 Data Modelling Techniques
Statistical and Rule-Based Methods: Initial or early curtailing of illicit transactions applied predefined rule connections and statistical anomaly detections. The frequently used rule-based systems employ manually crafted triggers or conditional statements (such as flagging a transaction of a particular amount or the address with a recognised high risk). These methods are simple to interpret but are not flexible and adaptive to the changing behaviour of criminals (Bolton and Hand, 2002).
Z-score analysis, density estimation and probabilistic-based models like the Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) were applied as statistical anomaly detection methods, identifying deviations from expected behaviour. These techniques assume a normal distribution of characteristics of the transactions (e.g. transaction volume or rate) and raise or flag outliers. However, they become less effective when faced with high-dimensional, non-Gaussian, and time-varying blockchain data (Cholevas et al., 2024).
Supervised Machine Learning Models: These methods use labelled data and have been extensively used to detect illegal addresses or transactions. Well-known examples of such classifiers are logistic regression (LR), support vector machines (SVMs), decision trees, and ensemble models like random forests and Gradient Boosting frameworks e.g. XGBoost and LightGBM (Hasan et al., 2024). As an example, Weber et al. (2019) used logistic regression and random forests on the Elliptic Bitcoin dataset. On Ethereum wallet features, Obi-Okoli et al. (2024) discovered that LightGBM attained an accuracy of 98.4% when a correct balance of the classes was given.
These traditional ML models are interpretable and can be used to make good baselines. Their main disadvantage, however, is the fact that they depend on designed characteristics and fail to represent directly the sequential or relational context within which transactions take place. In order to utilise these models, graph properties or features (e.g., degree centrality, betweenness, clustering coefficients) or time-related aggregates would need to be built (Agarwal et al., 2021).
Deep Learning Models (FCNNs and CNNs): Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) or Fully Connected Neural Networks (FCNNs) are deep learning models that have been used on structured transaction data. These models take advantage of nonlinear representations and have to be carefully designed in terms of features (Weber et al., 2019).
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which are more frequently applicable in image processing, have also been applied in blockchain by transforming time-series transaction logs to image-like matrices (e.g., heatmaps of transaction volumes or patterns). Ancelotti and Liason (2024) showed that 1D and 2D convolutions have modest improvements in predictive accuracy over time-indexed financial data, but overall CNNs do poorly on problems where time or relationship dynamics have no spatial structure.
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory Networks (LSTMs): These models can be used successfully to analyse sequential transaction data. Specifically, LSTMs remember long-range dependencies, e.g., instances where one repeatedly makes small deposits before making a large withdrawal, a typical layering strategy utilised in money laundering (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997). Elmougy and Liu (2023) and Li et al. (2022) have proven that inputting time-step features brings improvement to model performance. Models such as the TTAGN (Temporal Transaction Aggregation Graph Network) have utilised LSTMs in order to represent dynamic transaction behaviour (Li et al., 2022).
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): Since blockchain data have a naturally graph-like structure, GNNs are becoming increasingly used. They learn node or edge embeddings by summarising information about the neighbours on a transaction graph. Notable forms of GNN architecture are graph convolutional networks (GCNs) (Kipf and Welling, 2017), graph attention networks (GATs) (Veličković et al., 2017), GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017), and graph isomorphism networks (GINs).
Weber et al. (2019) were the first to apply GCNs to Bitcoin AML detection using the Elliptic dataset. More recently Chen et al. (2025) proposed the Multi-Distance Spatial-Temporal GNN (MDST-GNN), combining local and global convolution with temporal encoding, and it outperformed previous models by 1.5% in AUC-ROC. These models are very effective when it comes to the detection of suspicious clusters and relational dynamics such as rings of coordinated addresses. Although GNNs capture structural features properly, they are highly likely to miss the temporal dependencies unless they are combined with time-aware mechanisms. Further, the GNNs are difficult to train on large, dynamic transaction graphs with issues affecting memory consumption, convergence, and representation drift (Wu et al., 2020). Such shortcomings render combining graph learning and temporal sequence modelling valuable.
Dynamic and Temporal GNNs: In order to capture the evolving blockchain structures, dynamic GNN exploits the time or temporal aspect of the transaction flow. Alarab and Prakoonwit (2023) stacked or combined GCN and LSTM layers to develop a hybrid model architecture that processed temporal sequences of transactions. Pareja et al. (2020) introduced EvolveGCN, which learns the weights of GCN, implementing RNNs to memorise graph streams. These temporal GNNs are capable of learning time-sensitive embeddings that adapt to both network topologies and node dynamics or behaviour.
Graph Embeddings and Hybrid Methods: Alternatively, random walk-based graph embeddings (e.g., Node2Vec, DeepWalk) or signature-based graph descriptors may be used as input for classifiers. Some hybrid models combine CNNs or RNNs (for temporal features) and GNNs (for spatial graphical structures) and then combine the outputs for a final classification step. Such a combination commonly results in better performance, especially in advanced fraud situations (Chen et al., 2025). Even hybrid models are confronted with problems like model complexity, interpretability, and latency under extreme frequency conditions such as real-time fraud detection. Balance between detection performance and transparency as well as computational efficiency is necessary, particularly when it is deployed in regulated financial systems (Psychoula et al., 2021).
Unsupervised Learning and Anomaly Detection: When labels to be used are either limited or absent, unsupervised methods such as K-Means clustering, DBSCAN, Local Outlier Factor (LOF), Isolation Forests and autoencoders have been utilised. Pérez-Cano and Jurado (2025) applied LOF and clustering on Bitcoin transaction features to find out anomalous patterns. Although these approaches are useful in exploratory analyses, they provide more false positives and are in general less interpretable (Bouman et al., 2024).
Active Learning: There are studies that have dealt with scarcity of labels through active learning. Lorenz et al. (2020) utilised active learning loops on the Elliptic dataset where only the most uncertain or informative samples were queried. This approach attained a near-supervised accuracy utilising just 5 per cent of labelled data. A similar setup was used by Alarab and Prakoonwit (2022), where Monte Carlo dropout uncertainty was applied. In spite of its potential, active learning remains an understudied area in crypto forensics.
In summary, classical models like logistic regression and decision trees are much more interpretable and efficient baselines, but modern applications require the use of more expressive models. The relational dynamics of illicit transactions in blockchain networks require deep learning methods, especially those combining graph and temporal learning. In the following part of this subsection, the discussion focuses on programming environments and performance measures applied in the creation and testing of such advanced models.
2.3.2 	DM Programming Framework, Environments and Libraries
In order to instantiate the complex modelling methods covered in the last section, researchers and practitioners utilise improved computational frameworks and programming environments as well as specific libraries. These frameworks facilitate effective building of models, training, testing, and deployment, especially for blockchain data that tends to be volume-driven, time-sensitive and graph-shaped.
Scikit-learn: Scikit-learn is a core Python library that houses classical machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression, decision trees, SVMs and ensemble algorithms, including random forest and gradient boosting. It is driven by a modular architecture that makes it suitable to execute feature sculpting, model selection and performance assessment (Pedregosa et al., 2011). It has been commonly employed in the development of explainable baselines in research aimed at detecting fraud.
TensorFlow: TensorFlow is a high-performance, open-source, end-to-end deep learning framework designed by Google for building and deploying deep learning models. It allows mass training of LSTM, CNN, and also hybrid architectures and is easily applicable for sequential analysis tasks in blockchain analytics. The fact that it comes with a production-ready design, such as the support of TensorFlow Serving and TF Lite, makes it relevant in real-time fraud detection systems (Abadi et al., 2016).
PyTorch: The easy-to-use API and dynamic computation graph make PyTorch widely used in academic research. It is compatible with any of the main neural structures: CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs, and transformers, as well as the backbone of many libraries, including PyTorch Geometric (PyG) or Deep Graph Library (DGL). It is preferred because of its quick prototyping of deep and graph-based learning models (Paszke et al., 2019).
PyTorch Geometric (PyG): PyG is a PyTorch extension for graph-structured data. It has the best implementations of different GNNs like GCN (Kipf and Welling, 2017), GAT (Veličković et al., 2018), GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017), and GIN (Xu et al., 2019). It is useful for node classification, predicting links, and graph-level tasks, as it offers a great ability to scale to large blockchain graphs through mini-batching and sparse computation.
Deep Graph Library (DGL): DGL is an AWS framework with a modular API that allows building GNNs using various backend frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow. It is flexible with custom models and supports both dynamic and static graphs, which is perfect in the case of time-varying blockchain transactions (Wang et al., 2019). Nevertheless, PyG and DLG have specific disadvantages, including harsh learning curves and the requirement for comprehensive GPU-optimised infrastructure to scale efficiently. This problem poses a block to practitioners in a low-resource environment and highlights the demand for streamlined, scalable, and understandable GNN tools (Fey and Lenssen, 2019; Wang et al., 2019).
NetworkX and SNAP: NetworkX is a Python package or library made to perform exploratory analysis and prototyping of graphs. Although it is not a scale-friendly deep learning tool, it can give vital network measurements, visualisations and preliminary stage feature engineering (Hagberg et al., 2008). SNAP (Stanford Network Analysis Platform) is another high-performance platform that provides analytics over huge graphs and has found applications in transaction flows research (Leskovec and Sosič, 2016).
Jupyter Notebooks and Google Colab: These interactive environments make possible or support reproducible research, quick testing and rapid experimentation. Through Google Colab it is possible to access free GPU/TPU time, which is optimal to train computationally demanding models without having local infrastructure. The two platforms can do visualisation and live coding and include compatibility with popular ML libraries.
Cloud and Distributed Computing Platforms: As the data size in blockchain continues to increase, platforms like Google Cloud AI Platform, Amazon SageMaker, and Microsoft Azure ML offer scalable computing infrastructure to train and deploy ML/DL models. These platforms have parallelisation, distributed training and MLOps workflows crucial for real-time fraud detection pipelines.
The ability to apply the modern data modelling techniques to blockchain analytics largely depends on the accessibility of scalable and flexible programming tools. Frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow have now made it possible to create expressive models, alongside PyG and DGL, which now support expressive models on transaction graphs. Together with the scalable environments such as Google Cloud or SageMaker, such tools constitute the technical essentials of smart, AI-driven forensics of cryptocurrency systems.
2.3.3 DM Performance Metrics
The efficiency of machine learning and deep learning models in spotting illegal transactions on cryptocurrency chains has to be assessed using a sufficient number of performance indicators. They are used to evaluate how precisely and reliably the models can work under varying conditions, especially in the case of imbalanced datasets, sparse labels, and the need to get timely predictions. In this section, the most pertinent performance indicators applied in literature are reviewed, noting their feasibility and their limitations in blockchain fraud detection settings.
Accuracy: Accuracy measures the percentage of correct predictions (with true positive and true negative) of the overall number of predictions. It is used to get a general indication of the correctness of the model, but where there is class imbalance, it can easily misrepresent reality, especially in the task of detecting fraud. For example, in a case whereby fraudulent transactions comprise less than one per cent of the dataset, even a naive model predicting all transactions to be legitimate would have 99 per cent accuracy, making the metric not informative (Chandola et al., 2009).
Precision and Recall: Precision measures or calculates the fraction of predicted illicit transactions that are actually illicit (i.e., true positives / (true positives + false positives)). Recall or sensitivity is the proportion of correct sightings of true illicit transactions (i.e., total positives/true positives + false negatives). High precision minimises chances of false alarms, a significant aspect in compliance applications or settings, while high recall offers minimal miss rate in real fraudulent cases. These two are particularly important in balancing regulatory situations where the cost of a false positive (compliance fatigue) and false negative (regulatory penalties) tends to be high (Vallarino, 2025).
F1-Score: F1-Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, offering a single measure that creates a balance between the two. It can be especially applied when distributions among classes are skewed and when both error types (false positive and false negative) are important. If the dataset is imbalanced, the F1 score is usually a common metric used for comparing classifiers in AML and crypto fraud detection (Pérez-Cano and Jurado, 2025).
Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC): The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve is a graph of the True Positive Rate (Recall) and the False Positive Rate against the different or various threshold settings. The AUC-ROC score is a summary of the model performance in learning how well it can discriminate between classes at any threshold. The closer an AUC is to 1.0, the better the performance. In blockchain detection-based operations, AUC is often used to compare GNNs and the RNN-based model with various time windows or to address the different sizes of networks (Chen et al., 2025).
Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUC-PR): Where there is a high degree of class imbalance, the Precision-Recall (PR) curve is more informative than the ROC. AUC-PR targets the minority class (in the case of crypto forensics, normally the illicit transactions), hence providing a more accurate indicator of model performance. Studies have indicated that the PR-based measurement provides a superior depiction of the accuracy of models that have been learnt on sparse and high-risk addresses (Alarab and Prakoonwit, 2022).
Confusion Matrix: The confusion matrix gives a clear picture of prediction outcomes as true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. The presented matrix allows domain experts to understand the behaviour of the model and potentially show where it is vulnerable, e.g., over-reactivity to false positives or blind spots to elaborate laundering schemes. With its adaptability, it is very effective in operational optimisation of thresholds and alert priority systems (Monamo et al., 2016).
Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC): MCC is a balanced yardstick which considers all four quadrants of the confusion matrix and is preferred to use, especially in binary classification on an imbalanced dataset. It outputs a number between -1 (complete disagreement) and +1 (perfect prediction), with a value of 0 that represents random guessing. MCC has been suggested as useful in forensic research because it allows a more complete picture of the performance of a classifier than conventional accuracy metrics (Chicco et al., 2021).
Log Loss / Cross-Entropy: Log loss measures how accurate the assigned probability scores in each class are given by a classifier, and it penalises false guesses more strongly when the model is confident yet erroneous. It is often applied in probabilistic models, and it is also essential when a decision is not only based on the labels of the classes but also the probability or likelihood of fraud as well. Log loss is utilised in rank ordering transaction alerts based on severity level or confidence level in compliance systems (Vovk, 2015).
Computational Efficiency and Latency: Even though they are not predictive metrics, runtime performance such as training time, inference speed, and memory usage are essential in real-time or near-real-time transaction monitoring systems. In blockchain frameworks where the information on transactions is channelled as a stream, models should be not only precise but also efficient (Chen et al., 2025; Lorenz et al., 2020). As such, latency and throughput benchmarks are increasingly becoming a standard part of model assessment or evaluation in cryptocurrency settings. Another notable direction encompasses robustness metrics, which probe how the model can withstand a potential adversarial attack on the inputs. In a blockchain setting in which an attacker can deliberately modify the graphically presented or transaction pattern to escape detection, the robustness analysis assists in evaluating the practical effectiveness of detection mechanisms (Zugner et al., 2018).
When testing or evaluating machine and deep learning models in detecting illicit transactions, it is important to select the performance metrics carefully and make sure that they suit the specificities of the blockchain ecosystem, i.e., large imbalances, sequential or temporal changes, and hostility or adversarial behaviour. Although relatively subtle measures, like F1-score and AUC-PR, provide a detailed perspective on how well a classifier functions, others, like MCC and log loss, make methods more rigorous and reliable. Notably, none of these measures is sufficient; a multifaceted analysis or combination of these measures usually is required to sufficiently capture both the predictive quality and feasibility of operation.
2.4 Domain Area Analysis 
Detection of illicit activity in cryptocurrency networks lies at the border of several fast-growing areas, including data science, cryptographic finance, machine learning, cybersecurity, graph theory, and regulatory technology (RegTech). Each of them brings in different ways of looking at things, issues and research methodologies. To shed light on this domain space, one needs to conduct a profound assessment of the domain: not only to know more about the technical issue but also to appreciate the socio-economic, legal or regulatory and hostile complexities that shape this domain. This section critically examines past intersections of domain that define and limit the research problem.
2.4.1 Cryptocurrencies and Illicit Finance
The emergence of cryptocurrencies, initially created with an aim to enable or promote decentralisation and financial inclusion, has resulted in new channels for anonymous financial transactions; this has now posed a challenge to existing Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Counter-Terrorism Financing (CFT) systems (FATF, 2021). The fact that blockchain transactions are pseudonymous allows obfuscation, layering and smurfing mechanisms that weaken regulation. Even though an insignificant fraction of overall crypto traffic is illicit (Foley, 2019), the absolute value of illicit funds is large and increasing, especially in darknet markets, ransom payments, and scam-based fund-raising.
Another realisation is that the growing number of privacy-focused currencies (e.g., Monero, Zcash) and crypto-based decentralised finance (DeFi) protocols create additional sources of obfuscation and value laundering, including decentralised mixers, cross-chain bridges, and flash loans (Zhao et al., 2023). Such trends necessitate the need to adopt the application of detection models capable of managing heterogeneous transaction behaviour evolving over time and tracking multiple assets, emphasising the need for adaptive data-driven solutions.
2.4.2 Financial Forensics and Regulatory Technology
Financial forensics is fundamental to the detection of illicit transactions, and it aims at identifying, analysing and responding to suspicious or fraudulent financial transactions. Previously characterised by the dominance of rule-based systems, the domain now moves under AI-based methods that can detect anomalies in massive and fast datasets (Ngai et al., 2011). Regulatory agencies like the Financial Action Task Force (FATF), the European Banking Authority (EBA), and the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) are now encouraging the use of AI in the AML/CFT compliance systems.
A new sub-industry, RegTech (Regulatory Technology), is now gaining much attention. With the help of machine learning, natural language processing, and network analytics, it is able to automate adherence, monitor transactions, and identify suspicious patterns in real time (Arner et al., 2016). RegTech solutions are now making use of graph-based detection engines (e.g., Elliptic, Chainalysis, TRM Labs) to identify high-risk wallet clusters, laundering chains, and wallet links to sanctioned entities.
2.4.3 Machine Learning and Graph-Based Analytics
Illegal transaction detection is an intrinsic machine learning issue embedded in a complex network structure. The data in blockchain is a directed graph with nodes being wallets and edges being transactions. It requires employing graph neural networks (GNNs) and temporal sequence models (LSTMs, GRUs) to reach or capture the topological and sequential aspects of laundering schemes (Weber et al., 2019; Kipf and Welling, 2017). These methods allow identifying these suspicious patterns that cannot be easily detected based on static characteristics or features alone; examples include multi-hop laundering, recurring transfers, and extreme increases or spikes in transactional levels.
Moreover, the existence of malevolent actors that make active attempts to avoid detection systems necessitates the need for models that are not only robust but also explainable. Recently, it has been shown that hybrid architectures based on or combining transactional characteristics, graph embeddings, and attention mechanisms are competitive and most times even outperform traditional models when it comes to detecting more advanced fraudulent trends (Chen et al., 2025; Elmougy and Liu, 2023).
2.4.4 Data Challenges and Label Scarcity
Another issue that is peculiar to this field is the limited amount of labelled data and its quality. In contrast to common traditional areas where ground truth is readily available, in illicit finance, labelling would involve either court results (e.g. asset seizure or a criminal conviction itself) or the use of threat intelligence providers (e.g. tagging known scam wallets). That has caused and led to the emergence of semi-supervised, active, and self-supervised methods of graph modelling orientated to operate under sparse, limited or noisy labels (Lorenz et al., 2020). In generalisation, domain knowledge (typically known typologies in the context of fraud, forensic rules, or typology-based clustering) is becoming more and more blended with statistical learning.
2.4.5 Socio-Technical and Ethical Considerations
Lastly, the domain is highly intertwined with the legal, moral and social consequences. False positives in detection models may cause asset freeze or reputational harm, whereas false negatives can lead to regulatory sanction or systemic risk (Psychoula et al., 2021). Auditability, fairness, and explainability of models are not optional requirements but necessary conditions, particularly when the detection models become a part of a compliance pipeline that may be subject to legal challenge or scrutiny (Bücker et al., 2022). Furthermore, a globally decentralised environment makes it complicated and hard to enforce, as crypto transactions span beyond global boundaries. The dynamics of illicit transaction detection might be transformed as a result of the adoption of open-source models and shared intelligence (e.g., decentralised AML protocols), creating the new landscape in the future for detecting illicit finance in decentralised networks (Allouche et al., 2021).
The following domain analysis has shown that illicit transaction detection in cryptocurrencies is a cross-disciplinary and adversarial process that involves the application of AI, data science, graph theory, regulatory science, and cybersecurity. The study design choices made in this study, e.g., the use of GNNs, temporal modelling, and supervised learning, are motivated by these domain realities very strongly. With the changing blockchain systems and the evolutions of attackers, detection mechanisms should become more data-orientated, dynamic, and resilient to match technical and regulatory requirements.
2.5 Review of Empirical Literature
In recent years, there has been a rapid increase of empirical studies on the detection of illicit transactions in cryptocurrency networks, and it is likely to expand rapidly following the parallel increases in the adoption of blockchain and also the accompanying regulatory issues or concerns. This section gives a critical evaluation of seminal and contemporary studies, classifying them according to methodology, dataset and conclusions. Special consideration is paid to graph neural networks (GNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and hybrid architectures that are considered to be state-of-the-art in this field.
2.5.1 Traditional Machine Learning Applications
Pioneering research works used classical methods of machine learning (ML) with engineered features derived from blockchain information. An example is Weber et al. (2019), who were the first to apply supervised learning to the Elliptic dataset comprising more than 200,000 Bitcoin transactions that were labelled as either licit, illicit or unknown. They used logistic regression and random forests to extract features such as transaction time, value and aggregate behaviour of nodes to achieve modest accuracy levels (~75%). Although these models were interpretable, they did not make full use of the underlying graph structure of the blockchain.
Obi-Okoli et al. (2023) did comparative research and compared various classifiers, such as support vector machines (SVM), decision trees, and gradient boosting machines on the Ethereum wallet characteristics. They indicated that LightGBM reached an accuracy of 98.4%, which shows that ensemble models are effective when class imbalance is mitigated by methods like SMOTE and cost-sensitive learning. Nevertheless, their publication, as well as many others, was based on nonrelational tabular characteristics instead of relational transaction formats.
2.5.2 Recurrent and Temporal Modelling Studies
The temporal aspect of illicit transaction flow, such that the funds are shuffled between wallets over time, has caused growing interest in recurrent neural networks (RNNs), specifically long short-term memory (LSTM) models. Using the LSTM architectures on Ethereum transactions, Elmougy and Liu (2023) were able to identify fraud patterns on the basis of sequential behaviour. Their results demonstrated that by modelling long-range dependencies like the recurring deposits followed by subsequent lump-sum withdrawal, highly enhanced detection performance (AUC > 0.89) was achieved. was achieved.
In the same way, Li et al. (2022) presented the Temporal Transaction Aggregation Graph Network (TTAGN), which integrates LSTM blocks and GNN layers to learn the temporal and topological characteristics of transactions. The model was evaluated on a labelled Ethereum dataset and outperformed the RNN or the GNN baselines alone, which proves the usefulness of hybrid spatio-temporal modelling.
2.5.3 Graph Neural Networks and Relational Learning
Since blockchain data is naturally a graph of transactions and wallet addresses, GNNs have become the focus of most recent empirical research. Weber et al. (2019) modified the original GCN model of Kipf and Welling (2017) to analyse illicit flows in the Elliptic data set. They achieved better results with their GCN model than traditional baselines, capturing high-order dependencies within the chains and clusters of transactions.
Building on this, Chen et al. (2025) suggested the Multi-Distance Spatial-Temporal GNN (MDST-GNN) and proposed to combine local and global graph convolutions and also include a positional temporal encoding. Their model underwent benchmarking on 12 other classifiers and showed a 1.5 per cent increase in the AUC-ROC compared to the earlier existing GCN-based approaches.
Attention-based models have been studied by other researchers. Graph Attention Network (GAT), developed by Velichkovic et al. (2018), maps neighbours in the graph to different weights using importance and has since been used for transaction anomaly detection studies like Zheng et al. (2025). GATs are especially effective when it comes to pointing out dominant relationships, e.g., addresses that receive unusually large or frequent funds of suspected origins or sources.
2.5.4 Dynamic and Evolving Graphs
Blockchain networks are dynamic, thus requiring dynamic graph modelling. Pareja et al. (2020) presented EvolveGCN, in which GCN parameters are updated recurrently as time progresses. They evaluated their model on the continuously changing social and blockchain graph and showed that they had the capacity to monitor changing behaviours and new laundering patterns. Similarly, Alarab and Prakoonwit (2022) constructed a hybrid GCN-LSTM model capable of real-time capturing of changing sequences of transactions. When they experimented using a labelled Bitcoin dataset, they demonstrated an improvement in F1 score and early fraud detection.
These studies validate, successively, that temporal GNNs have an edge over their static counterparts in real-world blockchain conditions, especially when unlawful activity is implemented sequentially (i.e., placement, layering, integration).
2.5.5 Unsupervised and Semi-Supervised Approaches
Unsupervised learning is also an option since it is difficult to acquire labelled data. Pérez-Cano and Jurado (2025) worked on Bitcoin graphs using Local Outlier Factor (LOF) and K-means clustering. They demonstrated that anomalous behaviour, i.e., suspicious high transaction frequency/volatility, can be identified but with a relatively high false positive rate.
Most recently, Lorenz et al. (2020) illustrated that active learning could be effective on the Elliptic dataset whereby labels would only be queried for the most uncertain samples. They could match fully supervised models nearly at only 5 per cent labelled data, which is a serious milestone towards label-efficient fraud identification. This aligns with the fact that self-supervised GNNs are gaining attention, where models learn structural embeddings without external labels. (Lo et al., 2023)
2.5.6 Limitations and Gaps in the Literature
Although the recent findings have a large potential to transform the application of machine and deep learning techniques to detect the occurrence of illicit practices on blockchain networks, there are still some key limitations which are present in the current empirical literature. These deficits or gaps reflect the multidimensional issues described in Section 1.2 of the thesis and serve as the reason why a more synergetic, flexible or adaptable, and explainable detection architecture would be desired.
A primary concern strongly relates to the fact that there is an over-reliance on traditional financial detection paradigms. A significant part of available models uses traditional financial systems detection heuristics, relying on time-insensitive rule-based approaches or table-based features (Acevedo-Viloria et al., 2021; Kadam, 2024). Although such methods are interpretable, they are rigid and can be overcome by unpredictable criminal activity (Pan, 2024). According to Foley et al. (2019), the illegitimate players in cryptocurrency environments have managed to keep evolving at a rate that has now defeated logic programmed within such infrastructures.
Another notable limitation lies in the inadequate use of temporal and structural blockchain properties. Most of the research does not capture the time/graph-structured aspect of cryptocurrency transactions. Though some rely on temporal representations (e.g., LSTM) and others relational ones (e.g., GCNs), only a small group combines them, and thus the overall picture of coordinated illicit behaviours remains uneven (Kurshan and Shen, 2020; Wu et al., 2020). The links formed between transactions in blockchain systems are dynamic networks with non-trivial relationships between time and structure (Akcora et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022), although most models typically consider them separately, thus risking missing out on cascading laundering activities or structurally abnormal rings.
In addition, the issues of class imbalance and data scarcity pose a huge challenge. Illegal activity amounts to a very small percentage (often less than 1%) of overall activity on-chain and introduces high class imbalance, which negatively affects model performance (Weber et al., 2019). The consequence of such imbalance usually leads to either a high number of false negatives (criminal activity missed) or a high number of false positives (normal users flagged), either of which is very costly in the Anti-Money Laundering (AML) scenario. Although oversampling and cost-sensitive techniques can be applied, none of them is usually optimised to capture the speed of real-time streaming (Sanka and Cheung, 2021).
Lack of explainability in deep learning models remains another critical concern. Though deep learning models, and in particular, GNNs and RNNs, are capable of attaining state-of-the-art results in clear experimental conditions (Chen et al., 2025; Elmougy and Liu, 2023), a substantial portion of them are black boxes. This lack of transparency reveals their impracticality in operating in controlled settings where the outcomes of the decision should be legally justified and traceable (Lo et al., 2023). Interpretability is important and tends to have been compromised or sacrificed for performance considerations, and the percentage of studies suggesting post-hoc explanation mechanisms is exceedingly small.
Moreover, computational complexity and limited scalability remained obstacles. Hybrid deep learning models, especially those that incorporate LSTM and GNN elements, tend to be computationally costly and hard to scale to actual blockchain networks with low latency and great throughput, like Bitcoin or Ethereum (Paleyes et al., 2020). As a result, while numerous academic models are competitive on static datasets, they are unacceptable in production, where the inference speed of a model, its resource consumption, and the cost of deployment are important determinants.
The issue of data availability further compounds these challenges. There is an obvious lack of publicly available benchmark datasets that have rich annotations on both temporal and structural aspects. The great majority of studies are built on proprietary or restricted datasets (e.g., Elliptic, proprietary Ethereum crawls), which hinders reproducibility, generalisation and comparative evaluation (Obi-Okoli et al., 2023). Additionally, cross-chain laundering is a growing trend that is hardly considered in the existing empirical environments (Zhao et al., 2023).
Finally, there is an existence of regional gaps in empirical research. In crypto forensic analytics, the Global North has received most of the scholarly focus, even though cryptocurrencies are fast catching up in sub-Saharan Africa and other emerging economies (Boyode, 2024). Such unequalness restrains policymaking across the world and forms a blind spot in enforcement. It also hinders the formulation of homegrown successful detection and control strategies due to the inability of other studies to conduct a study on local circumstances.
Overall, despite the successful findings and methodological contributions of existing research concerning blockchain forensics, it is still conditioned by various theoretical, methodological, and practical gaps. These limitations will have to be overcome by the development of detection systems that are not only highly technical but also interpretable, scalable, and prepared for the contextual transactional processing needs of decentralised financial systems that continue to evolve as these realities progress.
2.6 Theoretical Framework
The theoretical background of the research is based mainly on two areas that are interconnected: complex network theory and anomaly detection theory in machine learning. These models provide the theoretical perspectives with which illegal activity in cryptocurrency networks could be meaningfully modelled, identified, and analysed. A combination of these theories enables a more structured view of the evolution of suspicious patterns over time and network topology, which are two of the most important properties of blockchain-based financial networks.
2.6.1 Complex Network Theory
The complex network theory establishes a mathematical and structural basis or foundation to represent relationships or interactions within a system as a graph of nodes and edges (Pósfai and Barabási, 2016). In cryptocurrency systems, one can consider each transaction or wallet as a node and transfer of money among them as directed edges creating a temporal dynamic financial graph (Akcora et al., 2018). These kinds of graphs are usually scale-free types of networks, meaning that there are a few hubs (usually malicious actors) with very high connectivity, having the potential to have a considerable impact on the network structure (Clauset et al., 2009).
This theory can be helpful in representing the relational interdependencies involved in blockchain transactions, especially those of criminal activities, like money laundering, tumbling, or fraud rings. Such operations will tend to manifest as subgraphs with structural anomalies, such as star topologies, cycles, or cliques, which can be revealed by graph representation learning and embedding techniques (Zhou et al., 2020). In addition, the complex network theory allows modelling network changes over time, thereby facilitating the identification of structural drifts, i.e., the sudden appearance of suspicious addresses or coordinated clusters (Holme and Saramäki, 2012).
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), which will be utilised in this research, are based on this theory and capitalise on it to discover local patterns, neighbourhood clusters, and inter-cluster dynamics, all of which define the process of spotting irregular items within the cryptocurrency transaction graph (Kipf and Welling, 2017; Hamilton et al., 2017).
2.6.2 Anomaly Detection Theory in Machine Learning
The second foundational pillar is anomaly detection theory, a sub-area of machine learning which deals with finding out unusual or abnormal patterns that severely deviate beyond expected behaviour (Chandola et al., 2009). In the cryptocurrency context, legitimate transactions are the majority, and only a small proportion of transactions are illicit, which means that the detection problem is fundamentally imbalanced and can fall into the unsupervised or semi-supervised categories (Zhang et al., 2021). Current methods of anomaly detection offer mechanisms to solve this imbalance, learning on normal patterns of transactions and identifying outliers without the need of fully labelled datasets.
To detect anomalies in this study, conventional methods of anomaly detection, such as clustering, distance-based methods (e.g., LOF, Isolation Forests) and density estimations, are complemented with deep learning methods, especially Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models. Such techniques prove to be capable of detecting temporal abnormalities, including unexpected spikes of micro-transactions or time-coordinated withdrawals that are indicative of laundering or scam activities (Li et al., 2022; Elmougy and Liu, 2023).
The hybrid GNN-LSTM model suggested in this proposed research can be strongly connected with the theoretical integration of structural anomaly detection (using graph representation learning) with temporal anomaly detection (using sequence learning). Besides depicting the intricate character of illegal activity, this synthesis constructs a comprehensive detection model that takes advantage of sequence anomalies of transactions, in addition to network structural anomalies.
2.6.3 Theoretical Synthesis and Research Justification
Since this study merges complex network theory and anomaly detection theory together, it is in a good position to identify the dual aspect of the illicit cryptocurrency transaction concept; that is, as a dynamic behaviour (because it changes over time as it continues) as well as a pattern (because it also occurs in a structurally encapsulated form). This twofold theoretical background ensures the creation of a hybrid detection model that reflects the complexity of real-life blockchain networks in that bad actors frequently employ both timing and structural camouflage to remain undetected.
Furthermore, this theoretical combination also explains the methodological transition from rule-based and shallow models to deep hybrid architecture, especially in fields characterised by high noise, label sparsity, and time structure.
2.7 Conceptual Framework
This presented research follows a multi-layered conceptual structure that is based on the use of integrated temporal modelling, graph-based learning, and hybrid deep learning systems to identify illicit transactions in cryptocurrency networks. The framework codifies the theoretical observations of complex network theory and anomaly detection into a practical model in which the underlying mechanisms through which illicit behaviour occurs are mapped, and how it evolves and can be algorithmically detected. The main elements of the framework are depicted below and explained in a detailed manner.
2.7.1 Blockchain Transaction System as a Dynamic Spatiotemporal Graph
The underpinning idea or foundation of the framework is a conceptualisation of a cryptocurrency network as a directed temporal graph in which:
Nodes are wallets, smart contracts or addresses.
Edges are timestamped transactions.
Edge features can be amount, frequency, gas usage or type of transaction.
Node features can involve or include address activity metrics, clustering coefficients, or PageRank.
This formulation combines the topological structure alongside the temporal flow of financial interactions as essential in determining illegal patterns from normalcy (Akcora et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022).
2.7.2 Behavioural Signatures of Illicit Transactions
The model makes the assumption that illegal activity, e.g. layering, structuring, tumbling, or dark market funding, has specific behavioural fingerprints along the time and graph axes:
Time abnormalities can consist of microtransaction surges, time-based drawbacks or timestamps in address recycling.
Such anomalies might appear as the creation of cyclic subgraphs, hub-spoke structures, or far more dense concentrations of transactions with low entropy (Wu et al., 2020; Weber et al., 2019).
These features can hardly be found independently or in isolation and are frequently necessitated to be modelled together with sequence and structure (Zhou et al., 2020).
 
2.7.3 Hybrid Model Architecture for Detection
To bring the above into operation, the framework incorporates:
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), and it will be used to learn node and edge embedding based on graph context (Kipf and Welling, 2017; Hamilton et al., 2017)
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) or LSTMs, and it will be used to model sequential dependencies across time (Elmougy and Liu, 2023; Li et al., 2022)
Fusion Layers to merge structural and temporal representations
Classifier layer that will be used to perform binary or multi-class classification of transactions or addresses
This integration idea is based on the hypothesis that to accurately detect these interdependencies, the two variables time and structure are vital to be taken into consideration or observation, and that deep hybrid models may yield better results than silo implementations (Wu et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2025).

2.8 Summary of Literature Review
In this chapter, an extensive and rigorous review of literature related to detecting illicit transactions in the cryptocurrency networks has been conducted. It started by presenting the fundamental concepts such as blockchain architecture, distributed ledger technology, and the pseudonymous character of cryptocurrency transactions. These definitions served as the foundation of comprehending the potential prospects and limitations of locating malicious activity in blockchain ecosystems, especially the conflict between transparency and anonymity.
After this conceptual underpinning, the chapter gave an in-depth description of the shift from traditional rule-based and statistical anomaly detection models to more advanced models based on machine learning and deep learning. The earlier rule-based systems were observed to be easier to interpret but showed limited flexibility when exposed to a dynamic threat environment. The non-Gaussian nature of blockchain transaction data and the fact that said data is high-dimensional posed a problem to statistical methods like z-score and density estimation.
Specifically, supervised classifiers such as logistic regression, support vector machines, and decision trees are some of the machine and deep learning techniques that have proved effective in identifying well-known abnormal activities (Weber et al., 2019; Obi-Okoli et al., 2023). Nonetheless, their limitations in modelling sequential and relational dependencies were the reasons why advanced neural network architectures have been adopted. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), long short-term memory (LSTM) networks and graph neural networks (GNNs) have superior capabilities in terms of capturing temporal patterns and topological patterns, respectively (Li et al., 2022; Elmougy and Liu, 2023; Kipf and Welling, 2017).
Of specific interest, the hybrid architectures combining the use of LSTM-based temporal models with GNN-based structural analysis have held significant potential in terms of enhancing the levels of both classification accuracy, interpretability, and adversarial robustness (Chen et al., 2025; Alarab and Prakoonwit, 2022). Although they have a lot of potential, the usage of such methods is relatively poorly explored because of the demands of excessive computations and the absence of comprehensive datasets with both timestamped and graph-labelled annotations (Wu et al., 2020).
Important development frameworks and libraries like PyTorch, TensorFlow, PyTorch Geometric, and Deep Graph Library (DGL) were also discussed in this chapter, which allow the implementation of complex models and enjoy advantages of scale and reproducibility. Evaluation metrics like precision, recall, F1-score, AUC-ROC, and interpretability measures were mentioned as significant features of measuring model performance in real-world AML applications.
The domain area analysis pointed to a growing pace of adoption in cryptocurrencies globally and regionally as well as in Africa, where there is rapid digital financial inclusion but weak regulatory governance (Boyode, 2024). This increases the stakes of illegal financial abuse and requires a context-sensitive technique of detection.
The examination of empirical research found three significant limitations in the literature. First, the majority of research utilises static table-based information and disregards the temporal dynamics of transactions, even though it is well-documented that illicit activity develops over time (Li et al., 2018; Kurshan and Shen, 2020). Second, most models fail to utilise the graph nature of data in blockchain and thus fail to capture valuable relational indicators (Akcora et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2020). Third, existing methods suffer in terms of interpretability and real-world deployability, especially where black-box deep learning models are used (Paleyes et al., 2020).
With the precedence of specifying these limitations, the literature has a strong relation with the problem statement that has been presented in Chapter One. Lack of an integrated graph-temporal model, uninterpretability issues, and regional gaps in research give credence to the importance and utilisation of the proposed research. The proposed study will make a contribution by filling these urgent gaps by designing and testing a new hybrid detection model built on both LSTM and GNN models with a real-world dataset (Elliptic dataset).
To conclude, the chapter has described not only the methodological achievements but also the unsolved problems in studies of illicit transaction detection within blockchain networks. It has provided a good theoretical and empirical backbone for the research to be conducted. The next chapter outlines the methodology that implements these observations into a practical and scalable detection framework.


CHAPTER THREE
METHODOLOGY
3.1 Introduction
The chapter documents or overviews the methodological framework that will be utilised in exploring the ability to detect illicit transactions in cryptocurrency networks, especially based on the Elliptic Bitcoin transaction dataset. The chapter gives a very accurate and detailed description of the research design, the data sources, preprocessing methods, model-building procedures, assessment measures and the interpretability process.
Considering that the nature of illicit behaviour in blockchain systems is very complex and transaction flows are both temporal and graph-based, a data-driven quantitative approach is taken. In this chapter cutting-edge machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods with the latest data preprocessing, feature engineering and model assessment protocols are incorporated. The grand objective of the work is achieving high accuracy and strong predictive power in recognising suspicious transactions in a real-life cryptocurrency environment, based on not only the structural feature of the network but also the temporal attributes.
This chapter is written and organised in a sequential order. It begins by establishing the research design, data acquisition, sampling and preparation, modelling, evaluation, and implementation workflow and finally ends by establishing ethical considerations within the chapter. By taking care to explicitly define every element of the methodology, this chapter operates as a template against which the reproducibility and validity of the findings to be presented in subsequent chapters may be judged.


3.2 Research Design
The study adopts the quantitative experimental design, where the voluminous and diverse transactional data related to bitcoin is utilised in building predictive models that can be used to identify illicit behaviour or activity. This decision is justified by the objective character of the data, the fact that ground-truth labelled (licit vs. illicit transactions) data is available, and because there is a need for numerical modelling methods that can handle the complexity, volume, and sparsity of blockchain data. The design is data-centric, and it combines computational methods of graph theory and temporal data analysis alongside machine and deep learning techniques.
The study is strictly guided by the positivist research paradigm, which states that the discovery of knowledge is based on observable phenomena and that they can be objectively determined using empirical procedures (Creswell, 2014). The paradigm is especially appropriate with the current study since it provides the possibility of formal hypothesis testing, reproducibility, and statistical measures of model performance. Moreover, it is consistent with the rigorous rules of science on which machine learning is based, where potentially significant explanations of patterns in data are tested by being given mathematical expression as an algorithmic learning process and assessed in ways that can be measured by performance metrics.
3.2.1 Rationale for a Quantitative Design
The use of a quantitative approach is appropriate because the research question is aimed at identifying important numerical and relational patterns that will enable a distinction to be made between licit and illicit transactions in the Bitcoin network. The existence of a large, well-designed collection of data with labelled results (licit or illicit) enables the use of supervised learning methods. In addition, the complexity of the underlying transaction graph and the requirement of generalisable and scalable detecting models compound the need for a formal and data-driven framework (Grover and Leskovec, 2016; Goodfellow et al., 2016).
Such design will enable the study to make statistically significant inferences based on data, employ rigorous performance metrics, and build machine learning and deep learning models that can be assessed in a straightforward manner. Finally, standardisation of quantitative procedures allows consistency, replicability and comparability among varying modelling methods.
3.2.2 Overview of the Research Workflow
The methodology has some vital steps in the pipeline:
Data Acquisition
The dataset utilised as the primary data source is the Elliptic Dataset obtained through Kaggle. It is a graphical and time representation of how real-life bitcoin transactions occur. The data includes partially labelled ground truths, directional linkages and transaction-level attributes (Weber et al., 2019).
Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering
The second step of the methodological pipeline is preprocessing, and it includes dealing with missing values, feature scaling, and conversion of the data to machine learning-friendly formats. The feature engineering covers both hand-crafted features and features based on the temporal and structural aspects of the network. They include time-window rolling aggregation, node centralities and graph embedding operations (Akoglu et al., 2015; Ribeiro et al., 2016).
Addressing Class Imbalance
Since the dataset is highly imbalanced (there are around 2 per cent of illicit transactions in the labelled data), several imbalance mitigation tactics are employed. These techniques include oversampling based on SMOTE and ADASYN, undersampling methods such as NearMiss, and training using class-weighted loss functions (He and Garcia, 2009; Chawla et al., 2002).
Model Selection and Development
Various models will be adopted. The process will begin with the application of baseline machine learning models, including logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost. They are then integrated with deep learning architectures and frameworks, including long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to learn temporal dependence and graph neural networks (GNNs) to learn along the structure of the transaction graph (Kipf and Welling, 2016; Hamilton et al., 2017; Veličković et al., 2017).
Hybrid Modelling In the need to combine temporal and graph-based knowledge or insights, a hybrid deep learning architecture will be designed. The model is a combination of both sequential and LSTM features alongside relational features and GNN-based components. The two streams concatenate their outputs and feed them to a last classification layer, enabling the model to be able to learn cross-dimensional representations.
Hyperparameter Optimisation
Tuning of models is performed using advanced optimisation algorithms (i.e., RandomisedSearchCV and Bayesian Optimisation). Validation performance propels the search, and time-step-aware data splits prevent data leakage mimicking actual real-world deployment scenarios (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012; Snoek et al., 2012).
Model Evaluation
The performance of the models is measured with the help of a variety of evaluation measures applicable in binary classification, especially when there is an issue of class imbalance. These include precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, ROC-AUC and average precision (AP). The confusion matrix is also analysed to determine the trade-offs between false positives and false negatives.
Model Interpretability
Post hoc methods of interpretability, including SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations), LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations), and others that provide model explanation, are intended to facilitate the trust building and provide explanation for any prediction. These tools can show the most significant characteristics in decision-making of a model (Lundberg and Lee, 2017; Ribeiro et al., 2016).
3.2.3 Experimental Design Principles
A number of principles are followed in the research design:
Temporal integrity is preserved by selecting the training and testing data based off the time_step variable. This guarantees models to be trained on historical transactions and serves to validate future transactions, which is critical to future real-world deployment.
Reproducibility is ensured by appropriately preserving code versioning, documenting preprocessing steps and utilizing a fixed random seed during experiments.
Generalisability is tested with the help of cross-validation and testing on chronologically posterior data segments, as well as checks on how well models run on previously unknown patterns.
Scalability is achieved through modular coding in Python using libraries like Scikit-learn, PyTorch, PyTorch Geometric, and DGL, which support big graph and temporal learning.
Ethical riguor is observed through anonymised data application and fair model conduct that will be expanded in Section 3.11.
3.3 Data Description
The quality, representativeness and structure of the dataset used in machine learning or deep learning research are always vital to the performance and the trustworthiness of the study. The major data source in this study is the Elliptic Dataset. It is an open-source, real-life set of bitcoin transactions, created with a specific aim to support the investigation of illicit conduct on blockchain networks (Weber et al., 2019). The dataset offers richness of transactional metadata, sequencing over time, and a graph topology, with which it is possibly uniquely suited to experiments in graph-based learning and in time-evolving anomaly detection.
The next subsections present a detailed description of the dataset in terms of the origin, construction of its structure (including file formats, feature definitions, and labelling scheme), time dimension, and temporal and structural peculiarities (e.g., the existence of imbalance and sparsity issues).
3.3.1 Data Source and Provenance
The data that will be used in this study is the Elliptic Dataset. It is publicly available via Kaggle and was originally published by the Elliptic Ltd company that develops blockchain analytics solutions focusing on financial crime intelligence. The data covers the flow of Bitcoin transactions and associated metadata over a time series of transactions that have been confirmed. It is widely considered as one of the most direct and detailed sets of data to perform research pertaining to AML within the sphere of cryptocurrencies.
Weber et al. (2019) stated that transaction selection and labelling in the dataset have been performed according to investigations carried out with the help of effective blockchain analysis tools and forensic heuristics. Thus, it offers stable ground truth to any supervised machine learning model that seeks to differentiate between licit and illicit behaviour.

3.3.3 Feature Composition and Interpretation
All transactions are represented as a feature vector, capturing both local characteristics of a particular transaction and aggregated statistics of its neighbours. These features may be subdivided into two categories:
Local Transactional Features (first 94 features):
They are direct attributes: the quantity of inputs and outputs, transaction value, balance changes, and address-specific peculiarities. Their application is especially valuable to traditional machine learning classifiers that work with tabular fixed input.
Aggregated Neighbourhood Features (remaining 71 features):
These features are developed as statistical representations of higher-order effects across the 1-hop and 2-hop neighbourhoods of all nodes, therefore combining contextual behaviour. They can, as an example, consist of mean values of transactions in the neighbourhood, the number of connections, and frequency of activity over rolling time windows. This feature set is critical towards capturing meso-level behaviour, which tends to be more revealing in discovering coordinated illicit schemes (Akoglu et al., 2015).
Feature scaling, transformation, and imputation methods are used to make sure that all features are on comparable scales and contain no noise or bias during training (see Section 3.6).
3.3.4 Graph Topology and Temporal Dimension
This dataset is structured, representing transactions as nodes connected through directed edges, thereby making it suitable for graph-based learning. The transactions can therefore be examined not only in isolation but also in context to their neighbourhood and their placement in the graph structure. Furthermore, every transaction has a time_step which allows temporal modelling. The graph is thus dynamic such that transaction patterns change with different discrete time periods.
The joint use of graphs and temporal structures makes it possible to use models that are based on spatiotemporal dependencies, including Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and hybrid models combining the two (Hamilton et al., 2017; Kipf and Welling, 2016; Veličković et al., 2017).

3.3.6 Data Challenges and Research Implications
A number of attributes associated with the dataset present opportunities and challenges:
High Dimensionality: The presence of large dimensional feature vectors (165 dimensional vectors) makes the model more complex and potentially renders it redundant, necessitating reduction or feature selection methods.
Sparse and Incomplete Labels: The unlabelled transactions are large in number and are likely to create bias in the supervised learning process unless it is controlled and managed properly. It also presents a chance with semi-supervised learning and unsupervised anomaly detection.
Dynamic Topology: The topology of the network changes with time, which means that it is required to employ the use of time-aware data splitting to prevent leakage of data. This is essential since it will help to simulate realistic deployment environments (Kim et al., 2024).
Anonymity and Pseudonymity: The pseudonymous architecture of the Bitcoin protocol implies that the behaviour of transactions instead of individual identities becomes the parameter upon which classification occurs. This therefore requires models that can deduce intent and risk based on structure and timing alone.
To conclude, the Elliptic Dataset offers uncommon, high-quality and research-grade data against which illicit behaviour in cryptocurrency networks can be detected. It possesses a clear graph structure, feature vectors and temporal labelling, which makes it possible to apply advanced machine learning, deep learning and graph-based concepts. Although the dataset is complicated by issues of class imbalance, sparse labelling and high dimension, it offers a perfect opportunity to train new hybrid models that can combine temporal dynamics and graph topology. Effectiveness, robustness, and applicability of the models that will be trained on financial crime detection will depend crucially on the careful design of experiments around this dataset.

3.5 Data Sampling
The role of data sampling is an essential part of the experimental design of machine learning studies, especially when dealing with domains in which it may prove technically challenging owing to class imbalance, label sparsity, or cases involving huge amounts of training datasets, thereby hindering the effectiveness of direct training. The sampling approach used in this study is designed in such a way that the training and testing data could be representative, temporally stable, and support valid statistical inference.
The major considerations with regard to sampling in this study are:
Making sure that there is a balance between licit transactions and illicit transactions in the training set
Sustaining the temporal integrity of transaction flows.
Preventing information leakage across training partitions and test partitions.
Adjustment of skewed representation classes through informed selection and augmentation.
The sampling techniques and the choice of sampling size used in the given research are explained in the following subsections.
3.5.1 Sampling Technique
Due to the characteristics of the Elliptic dataset (only a small proportion of nodes is labelled, and about 2 per cent of the labelled nodes are illicit transactions), a stratified and time-sensitive sampling method is applied.
Temporal Data Splitting
In the dataset, the time_step attribute is included, which categorises the transaction record in 49 discrete periods. The data is split chronologically and not randomly to reflect a real-world situation in which future transactions cannot be used in training the model. This avoids leakage of data and makes sure that the model generalises to new forms of behaviour as opposed to learning the existing surrounding correlations.
Let 't' indicate the time step for the transaction. The entire data is divided into three partitions:
The Training Set: Time steps 1–35
The Validation Set: Time steps 36–42
The Test Set: Time steps 43–49
The structure is in line with temporal train-test operations in event prediction and time series forecasting domains (Kim et al., 2024; Bergmeir and Benítez, 2012).
Label-aware Stratified Sampling
In each split, a stratified sampling is utilised to make sure that there is an equal representation of licit and illicit transactions. This plays an important role in maintaining a balance of classes as well as a fair evaluation of performance.
Let Dtrain, Dval, and Dtest denote the stratified partitions. Each of these subsets has the following properties:
Integration with solely supervised labelled data
Balanced sampling: SMOTE and undersampling to distribute the categories in the training set where they are not balanced (see section 3.6.4.5)
No overlap between transactions in partitions not associated with one another.
Handling Unlabelled Data
The transactions labelled as unknown are not regarded or excluded for the supervised training and evaluation but could be used in unsupervised anomalous recognition and semi-supervised graph learning during additional experiments (Zhou et al., 2020). These instances are kept in the graph structure in order to maintain topological completeness in GNN-based models, even though these are not prediction target instances.
3.5.2 Sampling Size
The original Elliptic dataset consists of 203,769 transactions (nodes). Out of this, 48,000 of the transactions have been labelled: 4,545 as illicit and 42,019 as licit. The remaining 157,205 of the transactions remain unlabelled.
Supervised learning is based on the labelled subsets that are sampled in the following manner:
Training Set (time steps 1-35):
The size of labelled nodes will be approximately 30,000-32,000, and the ratio of classes will be modified using oversampling techniques to have improved class representation of illicit samples.
Validation Set (time steps 36–42):
There are about 8,000 labelled nodes, with no resampling so as to produce a natural validation environment.
Test Set (time steps 43–49):
Approximately 8,000 labelled nodes will be used solely in the evaluation of final models.
In order to ensure that training preserves class awareness, the training set will be over-sampled with synthesis techniques like SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) or ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling), and the choice of technique depends on the level of imbalance sensitivity of the base model (Chawla et al., 2002; He and Garcia, 2009). In parallel, undersampling of the majority class is sometimes used in some experiments to keep computational costs manageable and bias the model toward attending to the minority class behaviour.
To conclude, the data sampling plan of the study thus considers a balanced combination of domain-specific limitation and technological best practices of machine learning. The application of partitions ordered in time, label-aware stratification and the use of imbalance mitigation algorithms makes the generated datasets valid to be used in training predictive models, and they do not suffer from common issues like data leakage, overfitting, etc. This strategy helps in a robust execution of performance evaluation and also helps to develop models that are robust, deployable and sensitive to real-world dynamics of transactions.

3.6 Data Pre-processing and Feature Engineering
Robust data pre-processing and feature engineering are fundamental to the performance of any machine and deep learning system. Raw data can hardly be used in its crude form in real-life use cases like in cryptocurrency transaction analysis. Blockchain data are typically high-dimensional and sparse (with temporal irregularity and class imbalance). Such difficulties imply that the raw data should be converted into a structured, correct and teachable form through systematic alteration.
In this study, the pre-processing pipeline will aim at making the dataset clean, consistent, interpretable, and informative. In this way, according to the data-centric AI principles (Whang et al., 2023), much of the modelling work is devoted to the enhancement of the quality of the data and extraction of relevant features, which are the most decisive parameters in downstream performance.
This whole process of the pipeline is separated into two broad categories:
Pre-processing, which focuses on data cleaning and data transformation.
Feature engineering, which involves the creation of new informative properties or features from the original and structural data.
3.6.1 Data Cleaning
By design, the Elliptic Dataset is a relatively clean dataset because it is curated (and to an extent labelled) by blockchain forensics specialists (Weber et al., 2019). However, intensive quality controls of the data were taken to ensure consistency, eliminate possible anomalies, and bring the data in conformity with the learning algorithms. Some of which are:
Missing Values:
An audit of missing values was done on all of the 165 features.
Although the dataset did not have any empty rows or null indicators by default, statistical anomalies (e.g., zero values in all time windows) were noted to examine or review them manually.
Conventional imputation methods were made ready in case any latent missingness was observed. These included:
Mean/median imputation for continuous-valued features
K-Nearest Neighbour (KNN) imputation for local structure-aware filling
Zero-imputation, only for semantically valid cases (e.g., fee amount of zero)
Duplicate and Inconsistent Records
A cross-check was made to ensure that all node identifiers were unique.
Edges in the transaction graph were cross-checked to provide a validation that both ends of the edge (source and destination node) were in the node feature list.
Transaction time stamps (represented via time_step) were checked for monotonicity as well as consistent assignment.
Such an intermediate data cleaning step guaranteed the graphical structure to be consistent and the features to be valid statistically and semantically in regard to learning.
3.6.2 Feature Engineering
Feature engineering is constructing meaningful representations out of raw data with the purpose of leading to increased learning capacity. In this study, statistical features as well as relational features are designed to characterise the behaviour, context, and effect of each transaction.
The features are categorised into three:
Transformation-based feature (scaling, normalisation)
Encoding-based features (graph indicators and IME indicators)
Sequential and topological patterns (graph and time-aware features).
3.6.2.1 Transformation: Scaling, Normalisation, and Standardisation
Machine learning models have a tendency to be sensitive to the scale and magnitude of input characteristics. In the absence of normalisation, the learning process may be dominated by features with large numeric ranges. These transformations were therefore employed:
Min-Max Scaling: This is applied to input and output as well as transaction value just so they are compressed into range [0, 1].

Standardisation (Z-score Normalisation): Acculturated to features that are a part of distribution properties (e.g., average neighbour balance, transaction fee). This gives zero mean and unit variance inputs in procedures like logistic regression or neural networks.

Logarithmic Transformation: Used on very skewed features like transaction values which occur in long-tail patterns.

3.6.3 Encoding Techniques
The following encoding techniques will be used:
Temporal Encoding
In order to support time-sensitive modelling, a positionalal encoding scheme defined based on the time step attribute will be applied. Characteristics gotten from time windows include:
Rolling transaction frequency at intervals of or within a k-step window
Average change of transaction value over time
Identification of long periods of dormancy with intermittent activity (e.g., bursty behaviour showing laundering)
Graph-Based Feature Encoding
The following graph-based features would be extracted as inspired by works in network science and graph representations learning (Grover and Leskovec, 2016; Hamilton et al., 2017):
Degree Centrality (in-degree and out-degree)
Clustering Coefficient
PageRank Score
Betweenness Centrality
Community Membership via Louvain Clustering
These attributes define the location, impact, and place of a transaction in the wider network and are especially crucial in identifying organised fraudulent activity.

3.9.4 Implementation Plan for Evaluation
The computations of all metrics will be made with the help of scikit-learn and custom evaluation scripts.
Confidence intervals will be calculated using bootstrapping by running each metric over different seeds in order to obtain a measure of uncertainty in model performance.

In summary, the evaluation framework is planned in such a way that it mirrors the ugly realities of detecting illicit activity in cryptocurrency networks. It ensures that models do not only have to be accurate, but they also have to be fair, interpretable and robust to class imbalance. The combination of threshold-independent measures (e.g., AUC), threshold-dependent measures (e.g., precision, recall), and probabilistic reliability (e.g., calibration) makes sure that the assessment gives a comprehensive overview of model behaviour in realistic deployment situations.
3.10 Implementation Workflow
The section provides a description of the intended operational workflow in carrying out the research project. The aim is to make sure that the whole pipeline to detect illicit transactions in blockchain networks is organised, reproducible, and follows best practices related to artificial intelligence (AI) research. The different stages have been formulated so that they would capture the challenges brought by the graph-structured, temporally evolving, and class-imbalanced nature of cryptocurrency transaction data.
3.10.1 Workflow Stages
The implementation will go through the following major phases:
Data Acquisition and Graph Construction 
The elliptic set of data will be obtained and transformed into a directed transaction graph. We illustrate this graph using G = (N,E), where N corresponds to transaction nodes, and E are directed edges that connect them together, representing the flow of value. To support sequential analysis, the temporal structure will be maintained by using the discrete time steps of the data set. Some of the tools to be used in formulating and handling graph representation include NetworkX and PyTorch Geometric.
Data Cleaning and Preprocessing
The first step of preprocessing will involve the transformation of missing values, dropping of duplicates, addressing data type issues, and ensuring feature homogeneity. Min-max scaling and z-score standardisation of all numerical features will be performed so that they could be brought to a comparable scale, which is fundamental in most machine learning algorithms (Zhang et al., 2021; Han et al., 2012).
Feature Engineering
Feature engineering will be performed in two dimensions – temporal and structural. Temporal features will involve time-step-based frequency of activities, burst patterns, and rolling window statistics. Graph or structural features will include node centrality, clustering coefficients, edge weight, and community structure. Node2Vec embedding methods would also be used to transform graph nodes into dense spaces that can be used in further modelling. (Wang et al., 2019).
Addressing Class Imbalance
Considering that the discrepancy between licit and illicit classes is huge, resampling methods will be utilised. The training set will be balanced with Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to oversample minority instances and NearMiss to undersample the majority class. Additionally, model loss functions will be adapted with class weights to penalise the misclassification of the minority instances more severely. These strategies will help reduce bias and enhance identification of low-rate illicit activities.
Model Training and Optimisation
Modelling will entail training a mix of baseline machine learning classifiers, deep neural networks, and hybrid networks. Implementation will be done using libraries such as Scikit-learn, TensorFlow, and PyTorch. Hyperparameter tuning methods, including grid search, random search and Bayesian optimisation, will be used. Cross validation is going to be combined with these techniques to prevent an overfit or overfitting.
Performance Evaluation
A suite of performance measures will be used to evaluate all models, namely accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC. Particular interest will be vested in class-sensitive metrics. Temporally aware train-test splits and stratified 10-fold cross-validation will be used to provide fair and generalisable evaluation. Quantitative assessments will be supplemented by visual tools such as confusion matrices and performance curves.
Interpretability and Explainability
The results of models will be interpreted with post-hoc explainability techniques, e.g., SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations). Attention weights of GAT models and gradients of GNN-LSTM hybrids will be visualised to see how structural and temporal features affect model predictions. Model validation will focus on interpretability, especially in light of regulatory and ethical implications.

3.11 Ethical Considerations
The identification of illegal operations in blockchain networks with the help of artificial intelligence imposes the necessity to introduce a thorough assessment of the ethical aspects of data usage, algorithmic decision-making, and societal impact. Therefore, this study has been conducted following a strict adherence to ethical principles in AI and data science development as well as financial forensics. This section provides an overview of ethical issues and how they are going to be responded to during the project lifecycle. 


3.11.1 Data Privacy and Anonymity
Although the dataset used in this research (Elliptic Dataset) is publicly disclosed, with the identity of the entities being masked or anonymised, it is still necessary to exercise due diligence to protect the confidentiality of the transactional data. The dataset includes no personal identifiers of Bitcoin addresses, complying with privacy-preserving principles (Narayanan et al., 2016). The analytical processes involved in inferring behavioural patterns will, however, not allow re-identification of individuals as well as pose unfair risk to entities.
In this study, no attempt is made to deanonymise transactions and attach them to real-world identities. All graph analytics, temporal modelling, and classification are constrained to metadata that stay within the ethical ethos established by GDPR (European Union, 2016) and other international frameworks of data protection.
3.11.2 Bias and Fairness in Modelling
Fairness in machine learning is of special concern when the classification problem is imbalanced, as the learning model may overfit and systematically make incorrect predictions on the minority (in this case, the illicit) examples. This biasness could cause false negatives, allowing an illegal transaction, or false positives to flag illegal activity when no such activity is occurring. The two outcomes have severe ethical and financial consequences.
Sampling techniques like SMOTE and NearMiss will then be adopted to rectify the class imbalances before training.
Weighted loss and focal loss (Lin et al., 2017) will assist in making sure that imbalanced class misclassification is penalised adequately.
To evaluate fairness across different timeframes and transaction types, post-hoc auditing of model predictions using confusion matrices and performance disaggregation will be done.
These methods are also in line with ethical AI recommendations offered by Barocas et al. (2023) and integrated into both development and evaluation stages.
3.11.3 Transparency and Interpretability
Black-box models in financial crime detection are ethically problematic because of their opacity and the possibility of unfair results. This study is specifically designed to include interpretability mechanisms on various levels:
Feature importance ranking on traditional models (e.g. Logistic Regression, random forest, etc.)
Attention mechanisms in GAT architectures
Post-hoc explainability using SHAP and LIME
Model predictions, particularly those identifying unlawful behaviour, will not be blindly accepted. Rather, interpretability tools will enable investigators and stakeholders to question the rationale of every decision. This resonates well with the concept of algorithmic accountability and the right to explanation (Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017).
3.11.4 Reproducibility and Scientific Integrity
Transparency and reproducibility are demanded in ethical research. The entirety of all experiments and data pipelines, including data preprocessing, model training, and final model evaluation, will be reproducible via version-controlled code repositories and standardised reporting using Jupyter Notebooks. There will be no use of proprietary or unreleased tools without adequate methodological disclosure.
Furthermore, this paper will follow professional ethics in scientific publishing, including prevention of data dredging, p-hacking or result misreporting. There will be proper reporting of negative results, model limitations, and failure cases in an attempt to promote healthy scientific discussion.
3.11.5 Legal and Regulatory Compliance
This study will be carried out in line with applicable laws regulating the application of financial data and AI. This consists of but is not limited to: 
The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
Financial Action Task Force (FATF) digital asset compliance guidance
Responsible AI frameworks from OECD and UNESCO
There will be no model deployment or operational use of the trained models in this phase of the research. In the case that deployment is considered as a part of any future research projects with industry or law enforcement, ethical approval and impact assessments will be obtained beforehand.
3.12 Constraints in the Field
Although the potential of artificial intelligence in identifying illegal transactions in cryptocurrency networks is encouraging, a number of practical and theoretical limitations are expected in field implementation and scientific investigation of this research. These constraints could affect data quality, model generalisability, computation viability, and the general adoption of the proposed framework. It is important to identify and correct these limitations to achieve a high level of robustness, scalability and applicability of research findings (Li et al., 2022; Weber et al., 2019).
One of the main limitations has to do with the availability of data, as well as the lack of high-quality labels. The Elliptic dataset, in spite of its scale, contains a relatively small fraction of transactions tagged as licit or illicit, with most of them being marked as unknown. Such incompleteness makes the implementation of supervised approaches difficult and constrains evaluation accuracy (Zhou, 2020; Weber et al., 2019). Label scarcity also creates a tendency towards the risk of overfitting, which diminishes the capacity of a model to capture generalisable decision boundaries. Partial solutions to this are to use semi-supervised learning tools like graph-based label propagation (Kipf and Welling, 2017; Hamilton et al., 2017), but since no extensive ground truth is available, benchmarking is limited. Furthermore, the dataset provides only a historical picture of the Bitcoin network, and this feature does not necessarily reflect the dynamic nature of illicit activity, especially with newer privacy-orientated cryptocurrencies like Monero and Zcash (Androulaki et al., 2013; Moser et al., 2018).
Slightly connected to the issue of label scarcity is that of class imbalance. Empirical observations show that illicit activities significantly comprise a minor portion of blockchain utilisation and legitimate transactions vastly dominate (Foley et al., 2019; Akcora et al., 2019). This imbalance tilts conventional classifiers to the most prevalent class (He and Garcia, 2009) and degrades detection sensitivity of infrequent yet high-impact illicit behaviour. Oversampling techniques including SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002), class weights, and loss-function modification like Focal Loss (Lin et al., 2017) may alleviate bias but are not without limitations and can themselves introduce noise. In addition, the proportion of licit and illicit transactions in live usage may vary in an unpredictable manner, requiring recalibration to maintain performance.
Another big challenge is computational feasibility. GNNs, especially those used in dynamic or temporal settings, are particularly resource-intensive because of the computational costs of routing the information message along more than a single hop in a complex transaction graph (Wu et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020). The Elliptic dataset in particular has 165 node features, which adds to the computational burden when considering combining GNNs and temporal models (e.g., LSTMs). Cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning in such a high-dimensional context are also prohibitively expensive, so experimentation is challenging unless resources such as high-performance computing are available (Hamilton et al., 2017; Ying et al., 2019).
Generalisability across blockchain systems also poses a constraint. Even though this research is firmly rooted in Bitcoin, other blockchains also have different structural and operational characteristics. As an example, Ethereum presents a smart contract execution, which leads to a combination of programmatic and behavioural complexity that goes far beyond simple transfers (Chen et al., 2020; Destefanis et al., 2018). Bitcoin-optimised models may thus not work when used on Ethereum or other systems with minimal adaptation. Privacy-orientated blockchains like Monero and Zcash conceal transaction records with in-built anonymity systems (Kappos et al., 2018; Möser, 2017), making extraction of graph-based or time-based features remarkably harder and thus constraining the generalisability of detection frameworks.
Besides these technical challenges, another constraint is the adversarial nature of financial crime. To avoid detections, illicit actors constantly change their behaviours; they utilise strategies such as transaction obfuscation, smurfing, or mimicking legitimate user behaviour (Kumar et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2025). This back-and-forth between detection systems and attackers poses a threat of model drifts necessitating constant retraining, and this is time-consuming, cost-intensive and also operationally challenging (Akcora et al., 2020).
Lastly, there is the trade-off between interpretability and performance, which is a severe limitation. Although deep neural architectures might perform better when making predictions, they are black boxes and thus cannot be trusted in situations where accountability is a requirement in the regulation process (Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017; Rudin, 2019). Simpler models such as logistic regression or decision trees will give clearer decision rules but often at the cost of detection accuracy. Post-hoc interpretability models, such as SHAP (Lundberg and Lee, 2017) and LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), provide partial solutions, but there are doubts about the extent that such explanations are adequate in terms of transparency and auditability required in anti-money laundering regulation (Goodman and Flaxman, 2017).
Overall, the suggested framework shows great potential in further developing the process of detecting illicit transactions within crypto networks, but its limitations are equally important to acknowledge. The problem of label scarcity, balance between classes, computational scale, generalisability across different platforms, adversarial adaptation and interpretability all limit its potential in the real world. Incorporating mitigation measures in the design and evaluation process, such as semi-supervised learning for prediction, adaptive resampling, scalable variants of GNNs, and careful explainability mechanisms, will be essential to not only achieving scientifically rigorous outcomes but also operationally feasible and ethically defensible results.
